Data-based Numerical Simulation of Buoyant Jet Flow

Step 1 — CFD reference, rCFD workflow, and face-swap-diffusion sweep · Step 2 — heat loss
Mochamad Bukhori Zainun — k12438440 Supervisor: Prof. Stefan Pirker Department of Particulate Flow Modelling, JKU Linz July 2026

TL;DR

Step 1 sets up the full ANSYS Fluent workflow on Windows 11 for a buoyant-jet test case, runs a CFD reference of the jet (its Reynolds number is low, $Re \approx 100$, so the flow is laminar), builds a recurrence-CFD (rCFD) database from it, and then replays that database to reproduce the temperature transport at a fraction of the original cost. We then sweep seven values of the rCFD mixing parameter $f_{sd}$. The best value depends on the metric: $f_{sd}=0.000$ gives the best mean temperature, while $f_{sd}=0.500$ gives the best thermal stratification ($CoG_y$). Step 2 then lets the tank lose heat at the top and tests how far the reused adiabatic database stretches: at the physical $h = 8$, and more visibly at a stronger $h = 100$, the replay captures only about a third of the cooling the full CFD shows, because it cannot reproduce the cooling-driven flow it never recorded. A field-based local diffusivity, read straight from the database, reproduces the hand-tuned $f_{sd} = 0.5$ with no tuning at all. A bounded negative diffusivity — an overshoot guard borrowed from flux-corrected transport, plus a directional weight — now sharpens the thermal front the replay used to smear: at strength $0.4$ it reproduces the CFD front width with no overshoot (§9.4). Under heat loss the same step makes the replay worse, which pins the remaining gap on the missing cooling-driven flow, not on the scalar transport (§14.1).

This page brings together Step 1 (the workflow and the $f_{sd}$ sweep) and Step 2 (heat loss). It explains the buoyant-jet problem, how rCFD works, what each $f_{sd}$ value does to the temperature field, how a field-based local diffusivity replaces the hand-tuned $f_{sd}$, and how far the reused database holds up once the tank loses heat.

Step 1 · done
Foundations — the adiabatic baseline

The Fluent workflow, a laminar CFD reference of the buoyant jet, the rCFD replay, and tuning the single mixing knob $f_{sd}$. Everything here is the groundwork that Step 2 builds on.

1 — Background

Running a full CFD simulation for a long time is expensive. The flow itself settles into a repeating pattern within a few seconds, but the things we care about (heat, mixing, stratification) may need minutes or hours to develop. Doing a full Navier-Stokes solve for that long is wasteful.

Recurrence CFD (rCFD), introduced by Lichtenegger and Pirker in 2016 [1], uses this repetition. After a short CFD run, the velocity field is saved as a database of cell-to-cell (C2C) shift patterns. The temperature is then moved through the chamber by applying these saved patterns over and over again. No Navier-Stokes solve is needed during this replay phase.

The catch is that the C2C shifts only move things around; they do not mix anything. To bring back the missing mixing we need a small diffusion step, controlled by one dimensionless number: the face-swap diffusion $f_{sd}$. This page shows how we built the workflow (Step 1a) and how we then tuned $f_{sd}$ (Step 1b).

2 — The Buoyant-Jet Test Case

The test case is a closed rectangular tank filled with water at $293$ K. A small horizontal jet enters at mid-height. From $t=0$ to $t=315$ s the jet is warm ($T_\text{high}=333$ K); after $t=315$ s it switches back to cold. The warm water is lighter than the cold water, so it rises to the top of the tank and a vertical thermal layer builds up.

How big and how fast is this jet? The tank is about $7.5$ cm tall, and the mesh is made of small cubic cells of $1$ mm. The jet comes in through a small opening of $5$ mm $\times$ $5$ mm in the middle of the wall, at a speed of $U = 0.02$ m/s. For water the kinematic viscosity is about $\nu = 1.0\times10^{-6}$ m²/s. From these numbers we get the jet Reynolds number:

$$ Re = \frac{U\,D}{\nu} = \frac{0.02 \times 0.005}{1.0\times10^{-6}} \approx 100. $$

A water jet only becomes turbulent at a much higher value, around $Re \sim 2000$. Here $Re \approx 100$ is about $20$ times smaller, so the flow stays laminar. This is the reason a laminar model fits this case better than a turbulence model.

We follow the temperature field with three numbers:

  • The mean temperature $T_\text{mean}(t)$, which tells us how much heat is in the tank.
  • The mean temperature rise $\Delta T_\text{mean}(t) = T_\text{mean}(t) - 293$ K, which tells us how much the tank has warmed above its starting point.
  • The vertical centre of gravity of the heat $CoG_y(t)$, which tells us how high or low the warm fluid sits.

Both values are written to rCFD_temperature.out at every rCFD step, and the same numbers are computed for the CFD reference.

3 — rCFD in a Nutshell

rCFD has two phases: preparation (run a short CFD, save the flow patterns) and replay (use those saved patterns to push the temperature around). The diagram below shows the whole pipeline at a glance — the animated dots illustrate how data flows between the steps.

3.1 — Preparation: building the C2C database

During preparation, the CFD solver writes, at every saved frame, a small list that says: "the fluid in cell $i$ moved into cell $j$ during this time step". This list is called the cell-to-cell (C2C) shift map. The database is just many of these maps, taken from the repeating regime of the CFD run.

3.2 — Replay: moving the temperature

During replay, rCFD picks a frame from the database and uses its C2C map to move the temperature field one step. This shifts warm and cold pockets across the tank, but it never solves the Navier-Stokes equations again.

The shift only moves things; it does not mix them. So a pure C2C replay would give an unrealistically sharp boundary between warm and cold zones. The next section shows how rCFD adds the missing mixing.

4 — Face-Swap Diffusion

After each C2C shift, rCFD goes over every internal face and moves a small amount of heat from the warmer cell to the cooler one. This is the face-swap diffusion operator. It is mass-neutral: whatever one cell loses, the other cell gains, so the total heat in the tank does not change. It only adds the mixing that the shift alone cannot give. This face-swap step comes from Pirker and Lichtenegger in 2018 [2], and Lumetzberger et al. in 2025 [3] later wrote it in a cleaner mathematical form.

It is controlled by two numbers:

  • face_swap_max_per_loop (default $1/8$): a safety cap on how much volume can move across a face in one sweep.
  • face_swap_diffusion = $f_{sd}$ (set by the user): the total amount of mixing per rCFD time step. If $f_{sd}$ is larger than the cap, the operator splits the work into several sweeps of size $0.125$ each.

For one internal face between cells $c_0$ and $c_1$ (the code in rCFD_swap.h first orders them so that $c_1$ is the hotter cell, $T_{c_1} > T_{c_0}$), one sweep computes a volume-weighted swap amount:

$$ S = \frac{T_{c_1} - T_{c_0}}{2} \cdot \min\!\left(V_{c_0}\varphi_{c_0},\, V_{c_1}\varphi_{c_1}\right) \cdot f_\text{loop}, \qquad f_\text{loop} = \frac{f_{sd}}{n_\text{loops}} $$

which is accumulated for both cells and then turned into an actual temperature change by dividing by each cell's own (phase) volume:

$$ T_{c_0} \;\leftarrow\; T_{c_0} + \frac{S}{V_{c_0}\varphi_{c_0}}, \qquad T_{c_1} \;\leftarrow\; T_{c_1} - \frac{S}{V_{c_1}\varphi_{c_1}} $$

Here $V$ is the cell volume, $\varphi$ the volume fraction (vof; $\varphi = 1$ for our single-phase water), and $f_\text{loop} = f_{sd}/n_\text{loops}$ the strength of one sweep. The same extensive quantity $S$ leaves $c_1$ and enters $c_0$, so the operator conserves the total $\sum_c T_c V_c \varphi_c$ — it is mass/energy-conserving. On a uniform mesh ($V_{c_0} = V_{c_1}$) the volume factors cancel and this reduces to the familiar symmetric form $\Delta T = \tfrac{1}{2}(T_{c_1} - T_{c_0})\, f_\text{loop}$.

$f_{sd}$ is a dimensionless number, not a physical diffusion coefficient in m²/s. The rCFD library does have a separate "local diffusion" option that uses a real Fickian flux, but it needs a per-cell diffusion field that we do not generate in Step 1, so we leave it switched off here.

4.1 — A different tool: the global balance safety belt

The same 2018 paper also has a second tool called the global balance safety belt (§2.4). It is easy to mix this up with face-swap diffusion, but it does a different job. A global error monitor tracks how much heat (or species) should be in the tank — the "active mass", set by the initial amount plus the in/out fluxes — and compares it with how much is actually there after the shift. If they do not match, the same face exchange is run in a mass-acting way — mass-reducing if the total is too high, mass-gaining if too low — to close the gap. So the safety belt keeps the totals right (conservation), while the face-swap diffusion $f_{sd}$ that we tune here only mixes and leaves the totals unchanged. In fact, the mass-neutral mode in the diagram below is the very same face-swap diffusion shown earlier — the safety belt just adds the mass-reducing and mass-gaining modes on top. In the code they even live in separate files: rCFD_swap.h for the face-swap diffusion, and rCFD_balance.h for the safety belt.

5 — Step 1a: Workflow on Windows 11

The rCFD tutorial was originally written for Linux. Step 1a takes the same tutorial and makes it run end-to-end on Windows 11 with ANSYS Fluent 2022 R2. The main changes were:

  • Replacing Linux shell commands inside the Scheme files with Windows equivalents (xcopy instead of a looped cp, absolute paths with backslashes).
  • Installing the Visual Studio C++ tools so that Fluent can compile the UDF library at runtime.
  • Adding clean-up logic so that the old libudf_rcfd_run/ folder is deleted before every run. Without this, a stale build from a previous crash would be reused.
  • Fixing an MPI crash that always happened at episode 272 of the replay (it came from a corona-cell sync problem).
  • Fixing blank snapshots: the contour plot had to be drawn before saving the frame.

After these fixes the full workflow runs end-to-end on a four-core laptop. The rCFD replay (600 episodes) takes about fifteen minutes; the CFD reference that the recurrence database is built from is far more expensive, at about 9 h 43 min (see Section 6).

5.1 — First rCFD replay at $f_{sd} = 0.75$

For the first replay we used $f_{sd} = 0.75$ as a starting value. The rCFD curves follow the CFD curves in shape, but $T_\text{mean}$ sits clearly above the reference. That offset is what motivated Step 1b: instead of guessing the right value of $f_{sd}$, we decided to sweep it.

9 — Discussion

9.1 — Why the best value depends on the metric

$T_\text{mean}$ is just an average over the whole volume; it does not care where the warm fluid sits. Face-swap diffusion conserves mass, but it also speeds up how fast the warm inlet spreads through the chamber. The real CFD needs finite time for advection to do this; rCFD with a high $f_{sd}$ does it almost instantly. As a result, rCFD ends up hotter than CFD during the warm phase, and the higher $f_{sd}$ is, the bigger this overshoot becomes. So the best case for $T_\text{mean}$ is $f_{sd}=0$.

$CoG_y$ measures where the warm region sits in height. Without any face-swap mixing, the rCFD field stays too sharp and the centre of gravity sits too high. With too much mixing, it sits too low. The best value is the one that matches the small-scale mixing in the CFD reference that the shift map alone cannot reproduce. In our sweep that value is $f_{sd}=0.500$.

9.2 — How sure are we?

The margin at the $CoG_y$ optimum is small: $0.069$ mm at $f_{sd}=0.500$ against $0.072$ mm at $f_{sd}=0.750$ and $0.074$ mm at $f_{sd}=0.375$, which is about four percent. The minimum is real, but it is also shallow. Any value in the range $[0.375, 0.750]$ would be defensible if there were a separate reason to prefer it.

9.3 — A physical alternative to the $f_{sd}$ knob: local diffusion

In §6 we picked $f_{sd}$ by hand and kept the value that fit the CFD reference best. There is a more physical option that needs no tuning. Instead of one global number, rCFD can read a diffusion value for every cell straight from the database (local_diff_on = 1). This per-cell value comes from the second moment of the propagator, as in Lumetzberger et al. [3] and Abbasi et al. [5]. The database has to store this field while it is being prepared, so it needs its own field-based build. We built one and ran it without heat loss, then put the result next to the §6 hand-tuned $f_{sd}$ sweep. If the database-derived diffusion is sound, the no-tuning local curve should land on one of the hand-tuned $f_{sd}$ values.

The no-tuning local diffusion lands right on the hand-tuned $f_{sd} = 0.5$ curve. At the end of the run it gives $T_\text{mean} = 307.49$ K, the same as $f_{sd} = 0.5$ (the two stay within $0.02$ K of each other for the whole run), and it sits between $f_{sd} = 0.375$ ($307.83$ K) and $f_{sd} = 0.75$ ($306.91$ K). So the diffusivity the database carries on its own matches the value we had to pick by hand in §6. This is the Step-1 result: local diffusion can replace the manual sweep.

The single number $f_{sd}$ hides what local diffusion actually does. Figure 7c puts the two side by side: on the left, the per-cell diffusivity $\kappa_\text{eff}$ the database carries, none of it set by hand; on the right, the hand-tuned global face-swap, which is one constant for the whole tank. Local diffusion makes the mixing strong along the warm jet and the rising plume, and weak in the still corners. These are physical diffusivities in m²/s, not a dimensionless knob. The database sets them as it is built, from how far the recorded flow spreads the content of each cell. Across the tank they run from about $7\times10^{-7}$ to $6\times10^{-5}$ m²/s, with a mean near $4\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, a few to a few hundred times the molecular thermal diffusivity of water ($1.4\times10^{-7}$ m²/s). Averaged over the tank it comes out near $f_{sd} = 0.5$, which is why the two agree in Figure 7b, but it is clearly not a single constant.

Why the two end up so close. In the propagator picture that local diffusion is built on, transport splits into two parts: an advective part (the first moment, the recorded cell-to-cell shifts) and a diffusive part (the second moment, which is $\kappa_\text{eff}$) [3]. The bulk numbers here, $T_\text{mean}$ and $CoG_y$, are set mostly by the advective part and by the global balance that holds the total scalar to its target; the diffusive part only rearranges heat locally. So the two runs match in the bulk as long as their total diffusion agrees, and it does: the tank-average of the local field sits right at $f_{sd} = 0.5$, the value the §6 sweep had already picked as best. The same split is why local diffusion does not help the heat-loss gap in §14: that gap is advective (a missing flow pattern), and changing the diffusion, uniform or local, cannot put back advection that was never recorded [5]. What local diffusion does add is the spatial detail of the mixing (Figure 7c, left), which it gets right per cell without any tuning.

We come back to local diffusion under heat loss in §14.

9.4 — Sharpening the front: a bounded negative diffusivity

§9.3 replaced the hand-tuned $f_{sd}$ with a value the database carries on its own. This section looks at the one thing no setting of $f_{sd}$ can give back: the sharpness of the thermal front. It started here as a concept; it is now implemented, validated and swept, and the results are below.

The replay gets the mean temperature and the position of the front right, but not its thickness. Two ingredients of the transport act like a diffusion. The cell-to-cell shift map carries some numerical smearing of its own, and the face-swap step adds more of it on purpose, because some mixing is needed for the stratification to develop at the right rate. The price shows up at the interface between the warm upper bulk and the cold bottom layer. Measured over the chamber centreline at the end of the run, the CFD front is about 6 mm thick. In the replay the same front is roughly three times as wide.

This matters beyond appearance. The quantities an engineer reads off a stratified field live exactly at that front: how much of the hot layer is usable, how thick the thermocline is, when the interface reaches an outlet. A field with the right mean but a smeared front answers such questions wrongly.

The deeper point from the §6 sweep is that no value of $f_{sd}$ can repair this. Diffusion only works in one direction: it can reduce a gradient, but it cannot rebuild one that has been washed out. Lowering $f_{sd}$ helps the mean temperature and hurts the stratification timing; raising it does the opposite. The method is missing the inverse operation. That inverse is a negative diffusivity: the same face exchange, run the other way, so that it sharpens instead of smooths. A normal diffusive exchange carries heat down the temperature gradient, from warm to cold. The sharpening step is its mirror image: it acts exactly where the exchange flux would run against the gradient, $(\nabla T) \cdot \vec{q} < 0$, and carries heat from the colder to the warmer side. Applied to a smeared front, it drains the blurred transition zone: the warm side gets warmer, the cold side gets colder, and the interface contracts back towards a step.

The overshoot guard. Left alone, this process is unstable. Pure anti-diffusion does not know when to stop: it keeps steepening until cell values pass the physical limits, producing water hotter than the inlet or colder than the initial fill. So the operator is only usable together with a rule that bounds it. The classical rule comes from Flux-Corrected Transport [8] [9]: no exchange may create a new local extremum. Every cell carries the minimum and maximum temperature over itself and its face neighbours, recomputed before each application. A cell that receives heat may rise at most to its local maximum; a cell that gives heat away may fall at most to its local minimum. The admissible exchange across a face is limited by these two headrooms, and the step takes only a fraction $s$ of the smaller one. A front can steepen until it is one cell wide, but no value can ever leave the range spanned by its neighbours, so no overshoot is possible by construction. The picture to keep in mind is an unsharp mask in image processing: sharpening, with a guard against halos.

Bounded negative diffusivity — one application per replay step
/* stage 1: local bounds */
for each cell i:
    Tmin[i] = min( T[i], T[neighbours of i] )
    Tmax[i] = max( T[i], T[neighbours of i] )

/* stage 2: limited up-gradient exchange, in energy */
for each interior face (c0, c1):
    hot  = the warmer of c0, c1;  cold = the other
    if T[hot] == T[cold]: skip               /* no gradient        */
    C_hot  = rho cp V[hot]                   /* cell heat capacity */
    C_cold = rho cp V[cold]
    head_hot  = C_hot  * (Tmax[hot] - T[hot])     /* J, room to rise */
    head_cold = C_cold * (T[cold] - Tmin[cold])   /* J, room to fall */
    dq = s * min(head_hot, head_cold)        /* moved energy       */
    dE[hot]  += dq                           /* heat moves         */
    dE[cold] -= dq                           /*   cold -> warm     */

/* stage 3: convert back and clamp (safety net) */
for each cell i:
    T[i] += dE[i] / (rho cp V[i])
    T[i]  = min( max( T[i], Tmin[i] ), Tmax[i] )

The head-rooms are compared as energies, not as temperatures: each side is weighted with the cell heat capacity $C = \rho c_p V$, and each cell converts the moved energy back with its own $C$. For equal cells this reduces to plain temperature differences; on a general mesh it matches the volume weighting of the positive face swap the step undoes. Four properties follow directly. The step is conservative: every exchange is pairwise, $+dq$ on one side and $-dq$ on the other, so the total heat is untouched. It is bounded: stage 2 respects both headrooms and stage 3 clamps whatever remains, so no cell can leave its local neighbour range. It is self-limiting: at a sharp front the headrooms vanish, because each cell already sits at its local extremum, so the operator switches itself off instead of over-steepening. And it is cheap: three loops over the mesh per application, negligible next to the replay itself.

Where it lives in the code. The rCFD library already sketches this idea. The face-swap routine in src/rCFD_swap.h accepts a negative swap value and carries a small // neg. diff. branch for it. That branch, though, bounds the exchange with the fixed limits 0 and 1, which is natural for a concentration field; our transported temperature spans about 0 to 40 K relative to the 293 K reference, so the local min/max bounds of stage 1 replace those constants. The operator itself lives entirely in the user file (rCFD_user_run.h), as the hook user_access_data_after_swap that the run loop calls after the face-swap smoothing and before the balance belt of §4.1 — after the smoothing it counteracts, before the safety net that re-secures the global heat. It is written through the public accessors only (rcfd_get_face_c0, rcfd_get_face_c1, cell data and volumes), sits behind an on/off switch, and with the switch off the replay reproduces the baseline bit for bit. The core solver is untouched — the same pattern as the heat-loss source of §10.1.

The expected side effect, and the cure. An anti-diffusion sharpens every gradient it sees, including the small irregularities that the replay field always carries along the front. The smoothing step normally hides them; the sharpening step pulls them out, so a strong isotropic sharpening returns the front thin but saw-toothed — the same trade-off as in an unsharp mask, where sharpening also raises the grain. This is exactly what happened, and it is what the direction of the exchange fixes. The waviness comes from the sideways faces: they sharpen ripples along the front instead of the front itself. So the operator carries a directional weight, $\cos^2\theta$ between the cell link and the front normal: faces across the front act in full, sideways faces barely act. The normal can be prescribed (here the front is horizontal, so the vertical) or taken from the local temperature gradient, which removes the last hand-set choice; we tested both.

The sweep. All runs use the local-diffusivity base of §9.3, so the mixing comes from the database and the sharpening strength $s$ is the only free knob left. One number first: with six faces per cell, strengths $s \le 1/6$ are strictly safe — even the summed claims of all faces stay inside a cell's head-room, so the clamp can never be needed. We swept that range, continued above it to $s = 0.9$, and ran both directional forms over the moderate-to-high strengths: twenty-one replays in all, each of 600 episodes.

The reading is short. Below $s = 1/6$ the operator is safe by a margin and improves the mean temperature monotonically (1.15 K down to 0.98 K against the CFD trace), but the front hardly thins: small strengths repair the mean, not the smeared front. The isotropic form buys the CFD width only at $s = 0.9$ and pays with a saw-toothed, mottled field. The directional form with the vertical normal wins on every metric at once: at $s = 0.4$ it reproduces the CFD front width (5.5 mm against 5.6 mm), more than halves the mean deviation (0.54 K against 1.15 K), and produces the smoothest end profile of the whole sweep — smoother than the uncorrected replay itself, because the sideways ripple-amplification is switched off. The gradient-based normal beats the isotropic form everywhere and finds the width at the same strength, but stays behind the vertical one: at a ripple, the local gradient points along the ripple's own normal, so the data-driven weight sharpens ripples as readily as the front. Where the front orientation is known, prescribing it wins. No run of the twenty-one overshoots: every end field stays inside 293–333 K, by construction.

The replay thus gains what §9.3 promised for the smoothing side, now for the sharpening side too: the mixing comes from the database, the direction comes from the geometry (or, where unknown, the data), and one number remains — the strength. Our working point from this material is the directional form with the vertical normal at $s = 0.4$. What sharpening cannot do is a different question, and heat loss answers it in §14.1.

10 — The Heat-Loss Model

In Step 1 the tank was sealed: no heat went anywhere, it just moved around inside. Step 2 lets the tank lose heat, and the place it loses it is the top. The water surface there is in contact with the room air, and warm water gives up heat to cooler air across that interface, partly by warming the air next to it and partly through a little evaporation. The top is also where the warm, light water collects, so it is both the hottest spot and the only surface open to the air. The bottom and the side walls sit against solid material that barely conducts, so almost all of the loss happens at that free surface. Restricting the loss to the surface keeps the part that matters and ignores the negligible parts, which is a reasonable simplification for a tank this small.

We write the loss as Newton's law of cooling,

$$ \dot q_\text{wall} = h \, \bigl( T_\text{top} - T_\infty \bigr), $$

where $T_\infty = 293$ K is the room temperature, $T_\text{top}$ is the temperature of the surface cells, and $h$ is the heat-transfer coefficient. The quantity $\dot q_\text{wall}$ is a flux per unit area (W/m²); the total wall loss reported later is its surface integral $Q_\text{wall} = \int h\,(T_\text{top}-T_\infty)\,dA$ (W), added to each surface cell as a volumetric sink $\dot q_\text{wall}\,A_\text{face}/V_\text{cell}$. For a water surface losing heat to still air the natural value is small, roughly $h = 8$ W/m²K, so the cooling is gentle. That is the point of the small value: a weak loss only nudges the temperature and barely disturbs the flow, so the velocity field stays close to the adiabatic one we already recorded in Step 1, which is what lets us keep reusing that database. (Whether "barely" is good enough is the question we test in §12.)

Two implementation details matter:

  • It is local. The mesh has a single combined wall zone, so we cannot simply paint a boundary condition on "the top". Instead we place the sink in a thin layer of cells right under the free surface, picked out by a coordinate test we chose ourselves: a cell belongs to the surface layer when $y > y_\text{max} - \Delta$, with $\Delta$ about one cell height. Heat then leaves only there, where water meets air.
  • It is in the balance. rCFD keeps a global "balance safety belt" [2] that nudges the field back to a target every step. If we removed energy without registering it there, the safety belt would add it back and the loss would cancel out. So the same energy we remove from the top cells is also booked as a negative source in that balance, which stops the belt from restoring it.

The sketch below shows the mechanism we are trying to capture, which is also the one thing the cheap replay gets wrong. Heat escapes at the surface, the cooled water there gets denser and sinks, warm water rises to replace it, and that slow overturning loop keeps feeding warm fluid to the surface, which keeps the loss going. Tap the parts of each tank to read more.

10.1 — The heat-loss term in code: CFD and rCFD

Both points above show up directly in the code, and it helps to see them side by side. The model itself is the same on each side: Newton cooling $\dot q = h\,(T - T_\text{amb})$ on the top cell layer, where a cell counts as "top" when its centre sits within one cell edge $cs$ of the highest cell, $y > y_\text{max} - cs$. What differs is how each solver takes the term.

In the CFD reference the loss is a Fluent source term on the energy equation. DEFINE_SOURCE returns a volumetric sink, $-h\,(T - T_\text{amb})/cs$ in W/m³, for the top cells, plus its slope dS so the implicit solver stays stable. Integrated over a top cell this is $-h\,A\,(T - T_\text{amb})$, the loss across the top face, and Fluent balances the energy itself.

CFD reference — Fluent UDF (CFD_user.c)
/* heat loss as a Fluent source on the energy equation */
DEFINE_SOURCE(top_heat_loss, c, t, dS, eqn)
{
    double x[ND_ND], cs, source;
    C_CENTROID(x, c, t);
    cs = pow(C_VOLUME(c, t), 1.0/3.0);              /* cell edge length */

    if (Top_region_initialized && x[1] > Top_y_max - cs) {   /* top layer */
        source  = -H_EXT * (C_T(c, t) - T_AMB) / cs;          /* W/m3 sink     */
        dS[eqn] = -H_EXT / cs;                                /* linearization */
        return source;
    }
    dS[eqn] = 0.0;
    return 0.0;
}

In the replay there is no energy equation to solve. The temperature is just carried along by the recorded flow, so we apply the loss to it by hand: each step the top cell cools by $\Delta T = -\dot q\,\Delta t / (\rho\,c_p\,V)$. Then we book the removed energy into the global heat balance as a negative source. Without that line the balance safety belt (the second point above) would read the missing heat as an error and put it back, and the loss would cancel. That balance entry is the part that is special to rCFD.

rCFD replay — user source (rCFD_user_run.h, inside user_access_data_before_shift)
/* same Newton cooling on the top layer, applied to the transported
   temperature, then booked into the global heat balance */
per_node_wall_heat = 0.0;

for (i_cell = 0; i_cell < int_cell_count; i_cell++) {

    cs = pow(cell_volume[i_cell], 1.0/3.0);

    if (Top_region_initialized && cell_center[i_cell][1] > Top_y_max - cs) {

        T_cell  = data[i_cell][water_temp] + reference_temperature;
        q_cell  = H_EXT * cs * cs * (T_cell - T_AMB);          /* W over top face */
        dT_cell = -q_cell * timestep / (cell_volume[i_cell] * density * heat_capacity);

        data[i_cell][water_temp] += dT_cell;                   /* cool the cell  */
        per_node_wall_heat       += q_cell * timestep;         /* J removed      */
    }
}

/* register the removed energy so the balance belt does not add it back */
rcfd_modify_balance_mass_source_one(handle, water, water_temp, -per_node_wall_heat);

This shape, a local source or sink plus a balance entry, is the standard rCFD way to drive a scalar on a frozen replay. The steel_RH_alloying tutorial in rCFDreloaded uses the same pattern to add alloy mass in an RH degasser; here we use it to remove heat at the surface, with temperature as the transported scalar instead of concentration. One small difference: the rCFD side reads $h$ from a file (H_EXT.in) at run time, so the $h$-sweep in §13 needs no recompile, while the CFD side keeps $h$ as a fixed constant.

11 — The CFD Reference with Heat Loss

First the ground truth. We re-ran the full CFD with the heat-loss sink switched on and compared it to the sealed adiabatic CFD. The physical value for a water surface in still air is gentle ($h = 8$ W/m²K), and at that value the cooling is small and easy to miss. To make the cooling, and the gap it opens, clearly visible, we turn the dial up to $h = 100$ W/m²K and use that as the reference case here. All numbers below are read at a common time $t = 600$ s, the span the rCFD replay also covers. We report three diagnostics: the mean temperature $T_\text{mean}$, the height of the heat $CoG_y$, and a new one, $\delta T_\text{mean}$, the average spread of temperature around the mean, i.e. how uneven the field is:

$$ \delta T_\text{mean} = \frac{\sum_i m_i\,\lvert T_i - T_\text{mean}\rvert}{\sum_i m_i}. $$

This $\delta T_\text{mean}$ is the mass-weighted mean absolute deviation (MAD) of the temperature from its mean. A closely related measure of spread is the standard deviation, $\sigma_T = \sqrt{\sum_i m_i\,(T_i - T_\text{mean})^2 / \sum_i m_i}$. Both say how uneven the field is; we report the MAD because it is less swayed by the few very hot cells in the warm cap, but the two track each other closely (for any field $\sigma_T \ge \delta T_\text{mean}$, and for a near-normal spread $\sigma_T \approx 1.25\,\delta T_\text{mean}$). The standard-deviation form needs only one extra term in the same cell loop, so it could be reported as well from a short re-run.

12 — rCFD vs CFD: the Gap

One check before the comparison. With the loss switched off ($h = 0$), the replay gives back the Step 1 result exactly: $T_\text{mean}$ and $CoG_y$ match the no-loss run with no difference, and $Q_\text{wall}$ stays at zero the whole time. So any cooling we see below comes only from the heat-loss term, not from a side effect of the code. The balance accounting that carries that term is checked separately in the $h$-sweep (§13), where it closes to about $0.03\%$.

Now the cheap side. We kept the existing adiabatic C2C database from Step 1 (no rebuild) and simply added the heat-loss source term to the replay. The key Step-2 result

The reused adiabatic database captures only about a third of the real heat loss: at $h = 100$ rCFD cools by 3.03 K against the CFD's 9.79 K (a ratio of ≈ 0.31), both read at $t = 600$ s. The same ordering shows up in $\delta T_\text{mean}$, where rCFD picks up only about a third of the CFD change. (At the gentle physical $h = 8$ the gap is even wider, rCFD 0.36 K against CFD 1.45 K, a ratio of ≈ 0.25, but the cooling there is so small it is hard to see; turning $h$ up makes the shortfall plain.)

Why does it fall so far short, with roughly two-thirds of the heat loss missing? Because we reused the adiabatic database from Step 1, the fluid flow in the replay is completely frozen: it only knows the motion that was recorded with the lid closed. In the real CFD, once the water at the top cools it gets heavier and sinks, and warm water rises to take its place. This is the convective overturning loop from Figure 8, and it keeps feeding warm fluid to the surface, so the heat loss keeps going. The replay does not contain that loop, because nothing was cooling when the database was recorded. So in the replay the top water cools in place: the surface difference $T_\text{top} - T_\infty$ shrinks, and the loss slows down. The missing piece is a flow pattern, not a diffusion setting, so we call it an advection gap. It fits how rCFD splits transport in the first place: the recorded shifts carry the advection, while diffusion only adds the small-scale mixing on top [5]. The Step-1 replay bias (~1.7 K on the absolute level) accounts for only a small part of the gap; the frozen flow is the main cause. This is the limit of reusing an adiabatic database and adding a source term on top, and it is the result Step 2 was meant to show. It is also a known soft spot of the method: applied to heat transfer in a fluidised bed, transport-based rCFD smeared out the sharp, localised heat-transfer layer that the flow drives [2]. Our under-captured cold cap is the buoyant-jet version of the same difficulty, reproducing a localised, flow-driven thermal structure on a frozen flow.

What the gap really measures

It helps to read the shortfall as a test of database reuse, not just a heat-loss result. The recurrence database was recorded once, from the adiabatic flow, and Step 2 reuses it under a boundary condition it never saw. The roughly two-thirds shortfall at $h = 100$ (wider at the gentle $h = 8$) is then a direct measure of how far that one database stretches: the physics it did record (the advection in the shift maps) still carries over, while the physics it never recorded (the cooling-driven down-flow) cannot appear no matter how the source term is tuned. So the number to keep from Step 2 is not the cooling itself but how much of it a reused adiabatic database can recover, which §15 takes up by rebuilding the database from a heat-loss run, and Step 3 by going multi-state.

The shortfall is not a fixed offset; it grows as the run goes on. The figure below tracks the cooling itself over time at $h = 100$: how much each method has cooled relative to its own adiabatic baseline.

14 — Does Local Diffusion Help?

In §9.3 local diffusion already matched the hand-tuned face-swap on the adiabatic case: it reads a diffusion field per cell from the database (local_diff_on = 1) instead of tuning $f_{sd}$, following Lumetzberger et al. [3] and Abbasi et al. [5]. The question now is whether this same no-tuning path also helps once heat loss is on. We ran it at the reference $h = 100$ on the same 600-frame field-based database. The result is clean, but for heat loss it does not help much:

adiabatic308.90307.49307.49

The first column is the same replay with the diffusion model switched off. Turning it on, by local diffusion or by the global face-swap, lowers $T_\text{mean}$ from that baseline by $1.4$ K in the adiabatic case and by $1.9$ K under heat loss. The lowering is a little larger under heat loss because the same mixing also spreads heat into the cooled top layer, where a bit more of it leaves through the surface. So the diffusion model is active and doing real work.

Both with and without heat loss, local diffusion and the global face-swap come out identical, with the same $T_\text{mean}$, $CoG_y$, and $\delta T_\text{mean}$ (the cooling is $3.03$ K either way). That makes sense once you look at what local diffusion is. It comes from the second moment of the propagator, $k^{(2c)}$, in Lumetzberger et al. [3], a per-cell measure of how far cell content spreads, which sets a local diffusion coefficient. So it controls how heat smears out locally and cleans up the numerical smoothing inside the flow we already have. What it cannot do is invent the missing down-flow vectors, since the sinking cooled parcels live in a flow field that was never recorded. The local diffusivity field itself (Figure 7c) is the same under heat loss as in the adiabatic case, because it is read from the same recorded flow: the frozen database cannot hand the heat-loss run a diffusivity, or a flow, that adapts to the cooling. Switching the diffusion model only rearranges heat within a frozen flow; it cannot add a flow pattern that is not there. So local diffusion is a useful refinement, because it lets us drop the hand-tuned $f_{sd}$ knob for something physical, but it does not close the heat-loss gap.

One idea for closing the gap → §15

If the problem is a missing flow pattern, the natural thing to try is to give rCFD a database that already contains it: rebuild the C2C database from a CFD run that itself has heat loss, so the cooling-driven down-flows are recorded into the patterns. The trade-off is that such a database is tied to that one heat-loss setting, swapping rCFD's "build once, reuse everywhere" strength for accuracy. We test it next, in §15.

14.1 — Sharpening under heat loss: a clean negative result

§9.4 gave the replay a sharpening step that repairs the smeared front on the adiabatic case. The natural next question: does it also help under heat loss? We switched the §10 heat-loss sink on at $h = 100$, kept everything else of §9.4 — the same adiabatic local-diffusivity database, the same working point — and ran three replays: sharpening off, isotropic at $s = 0.4$, directional at $s = 0.4$. With the sharpening off, the run reproduces the heat-loss replay of the table above digit for digit ($304.46$ K), so the comparison sits on a verified base.

The sharpening makes the heat-loss replay worse, not better: the captured cooling drops from 31% to 12–13%. That is a negative result, but it is the clean kind, because it says precisely where the remaining error lives.

Why? The sink at the top surface scales with the film temperature, $q = h\,A\,(T - T_\text{amb})$. In the CFD, the cooled surface water sinks in thin cold streaks, the bulk overturns, and warm water keeps arriving at the surface to be cooled in turn — the flux stays alive. The replay's flow is the frozen adiabatic loop, and a frozen flow does not react to the new cold fluid: nothing sinks. The plain replay still cools noticeably, because its numerical smearing keeps exchanging fluid across the top layer — cold down, warm up. That smearing, the very thing §9.4 is built to remove, had been quietly standing in for the missing vertical transport. The sharpened replays remove it: a thin, clean cold film settles on the top layer, its temperature falls towards $T_\text{amb}$, and the wall flux quenches itself. The hot bulk below stays hot.

Put next to §14, this sorts the two errors of the heat-loss replay cleanly. The smeared front is a numerical error, and the scalar side now fixes it (§9.4). The missing cooling is a transport error, and no scalar-side operator can fix it — not the diffusion model (§14), and not its inverse (this section). Both experiments point at the same place: the flow states in the database. That is exactly the motivation for §15 and §16 — bring the cooling-driven motion into the recorded patterns; the sharpening step then has the job it is good at, keeping the resulting fronts and low-temperature streaks sharp instead of smeared.

15 — Rebuilding the Database with Heat Loss

Section 12 pinned the gap on a missing flow pattern: the adiabatic database never saw the cooling-driven down-flow, so the replay cannot make it. The obvious fix is to build the database from a run that already has the heat loss in it, so those down-flows are recorded from the start. We did that. We re-ran the CFD with the $h = 100$ wall loss switched on, recorded a fresh 600-frame C2C database from it (the non-adiabatic database), and replayed the same heat-loss case on it. Everything else was held fixed: $f_{sd} = 0.5$, $h = 100$, read at $t = 600$ s.

The result is the surprise of Step 2. The rebuilt database does not close the gap. Measured the fair way, the cooling each method reaches from its own no-loss baseline, it does a little worse:

So the rebuilt database recovers about $26\%$ of the cooling, against $31\%$ for the reused adiabatic one. Rebuilding did not help; if anything it cost a few points. That is worth sitting with, because it is the opposite of what the rebuild was meant to do.

The reason is in how the non-adiabatic run settles. With the loss on, the jet runs colder, so it is less buoyant, so its circulation is weaker. The database records that weaker loop. A weaker loop carries less warm fluid up to the surface where the loss acts, so the frozen flow, even though it was born under cooling, moves less heat to where it can leave. The deeper point is that the real cooling is a transient. The flow starts hot and vigorous and slows as the tank cools, passing through a whole sequence of regimes. A single database freezes one of them: the adiabatic one freezes the hot, vigorous end, the non-adiabatic one freezes the cold, weak end. Neither holds the sequence, and the sequence is what the heat loss needs.

One thing the rebuild does improve is the evenness of the field. $\delta T_\text{mean}$ drops from $10.78$ K on the adiabatic database to $9.05$ K, a step toward the CFD's $7.32$ K, because the recorded cooled flow is already better mixed. Pushing the face-swap up to $f_{sd} = 0.75$ takes it a little further, to $\delta T_\text{mean} = 8.36$ K and $T_\text{mean} = 303.01$ K. But that is a sensitivity check, not a fix: more face-swap is more diffusion, and the gap is advective, so the cooling saturates well short of the CFD, the same lesson as the $h$-sweep in §13. We keep $f_{sd} = 0.5$ as the value to report, the one anchored by the local-diffusion field in §9.3.

15.1 — How the heat-loss database is built

Only one thing changes between the two databases, and it is where the heat loss acts. For the adiabatic database (Step 1), the prep CFD is sealed: it records the buoyant flow with no loss, and the wall loss is added only later, at replay, as the passive scalar sink from §10.1. For the non-adiabatic database, the same loss is switched on inside the prep CFD itself. It is the CFD-side source term from §10.1, a volumetric sink in the top cell layer, $\dot q = -h\,(T - T_\infty)/\Delta x$ (W/m³), added to the energy equation rather than to a passive scalar. So the cooling is felt by the real temperature, which changes the density, which changes the buoyancy, which changes the flow. The recorder then writes that cooled flow into the C2C maps. Everything else is identical to Step 1: same mesh, same 600 frames, same recurrence settings, only the recorded flow now carries the cooling-driven down-flow.

At replay the heat loss is applied once more, the same passive scalar sink used on the adiabatic database, so the table above is a like-for-like test: both runs drive the temperature scalar with the identical $h = 100$ term, and the only difference is whether the recorded flow underneath it ever felt the cooling. That is what makes the result clean to read — the 26%-against-31% difference is down to the flow database alone, not to the heat-loss term.

16 — Step 3: Multi-State Database Experiments

Step 2 settled one thing: a single database freezes a single flow regime, and that is the ceiling, whether the regime is the adiabatic one (§12) or one rebuilt under heat loss (§15). The heat-loss transient, though, runs through many regimes, from the hot vigorous start to the cold slow end. A multi-state database stores several of those regimes from the recorded flow and lets the replay move between them as the tank cools, which loosens the frozen-flow limit a single database cannot escape. Step 3 is where we would build one and test it.

Directions we are considering:

  • Build a multi-state database. Split the recorded flow into several states keyed to the changing circulation, so the replay has more than one regime to draw on instead of a single frozen loop.
  • Replay heat loss across the states. Run the same $h = 100$ case on the multi-state database and read the three metrics ($T_\text{mean}$, $CoG_y$, $\delta T_\text{mean}$) against the CFD reference (9.79 K of cooling at $h = 100$), to see how much of the gap the extra states recover.
  • Weigh the cost. More states mean a larger database and more bookkeeping at replay; the question is how much missing cooling they buy back for that price.

Not started yet; these are proposed directions only, and any results will be added here as the work is done.
