18 — Giving the Replay Its Transport

§17 ends on a "no". Seven local rules were tried and none of them touched the one thing that stayed wrong, and the reason turned out to be structural: every one of them takes heat from a cell and gives the same heat to its neighbour, so none of them can change how much heat leaves the tank. If the missing piece really is transport, then the only honest move is to supply transport rather than approximate it.

Two things had to be sorted out first.

18.1 — The balance hands some of it back

Here is something that does not look right until you check it. The replay's wall term removes 1930 J — more than the same formula gives on the reference's own field. And yet the tank only ends up 893 J colder than the same replay with no sink at all. About a thousand joules come back, and the only thing in the run that can put heat into the field is the global balance correction.

The mechanism is a feedback loop. The outlet part of the balance target is built from the temperature of the replay's own outlet channel. Cool the tank down, and the replay exports less; export less, and the target goes up; the target goes up, and the correction makes up the difference by pushing heat across the faces with the biggest temperature difference — which is the front. In the reference the same cooling does the opposite, because the denser fluid sinks and brings the front to the outlet sooner.

How big is it? Switch the correction off and see. Nothing then holds the total, so the run is not physical, but it puts a number on it.

From 47 % to 75 % with nothing added. The correction was quietly returning 2.27 K.

That is a diagnosis, not a fix. Without the correction the heterogeneity gets worse, 2.42 to 3.82 K, and the centre of gravity nearly doubles its error, because now nothing enforces conservation at all. The method as published puts the balance on compartments, not on one domain, and this working directory uses the coarsest choice there is: one compartment for the whole tank. Splitting it is the proper repair, and it has not been done yet.

18.2 — A transport that was recorded, not invented

The database can hold more than the map and the diffusivity. During the same preparation run the CFD also writes, frame by frame and cell by cell,

$F = \rho\,c_p\,v\,(T - \bar{T}_\text{band})$

the vertical convective heat flux carried by the local temperature anomaly, with $\bar{T}_\text{band}$ the mean over the horizontal band one cell layer thick. In the replay the file for the frame being played is read back and its divergence applied face by face, under the same head-room limiter the sharpening step already uses. Nothing is tuned here at all.

It lives in data/flux/flux_<frame>_<phase>_<partition>, 2401 files, laid out like the recorded diffusivity. The writer is user_set_rec_vars in rCFD_user_prep_local.h, which the library calls once per recorded frame — deliberately not a CFD-side UDF, because those run on the solver's schedule and would count frames differently from the diffusivity.

The heterogeneity is the measure §17 could not shift. The flux halves it in a single run, with no gain and no operator. That is about as direct a confirmation as you get that the leftover really was transport.

One detail worth knowing: it works mostly through the outlet, not the wall. With the flux on, the wall term goes up only from 1930 to 2017 J — 87 J — while the tank loses 437 J more. The flux carries heat downwards, the front reaches the outlet sooner, and more heat leaves that way.

18.3 — The operator was simply being driven too hard

The family was tried on the flux once, at the strength that had won without it, and every single member came out worse. That test does not stand. The family stops being six competitors and becomes one operator whose only real setting is a strength that has to be matched to whatever else is already moving heat.

Neither the gain nor the flux is free, and they are not interchangeable. Read across the row, not down one column.

Nothing here dominates. The second row reaches the right end state. The third reproduces the structure of the field on the way there and gives up a bit of the end state to do it. The fourth is the most even of the lot, with nothing above 0.68. Which one you prefer depends on what you want to claim — and the honest way to put it is that the end temperature can be reached by the wrong route, while the structure cannot.

18.5 — The price, and three attempts to dodge it

That stepped plume is not a rendering glitch. In the jet the departure from the band mean is far bigger than anything in the layer the flux was meant to move, so the recorded flux there is large and the per-cell limiter shifts it in discrete lumps. None of the four measures notices: it lives in the filling phase and in a thin feature, and the metrics that would catch it are averages. It took watching the frames.

Three ways of taming it were tried. All three cost the heterogeneity by roughly the same amount.

Restrict the term to a band under the lid, where the film forms and the jet is not. That gives 0.31 K at 30 mm, 0.73 at 20 mm and 1.84 at 10 mm, against 0.26 unrestricted. It also brings its own defect: the term switches off at a fixed height, so the plume changes behaviour as it crosses and the field ends up with a seam through it.

Put a soft ceiling on the flux in units of its own mean magnitude, $F \rightarrow F_c \tanh(F/F_c)$. No seam, but it costs 0.55 K at $F_c = 5\overline{|F|}$, 0.76 at three and 0.94 at two.

Apply that ceiling only while the inflow is hot, so the film is left alone afterwards. Still costs: 0.71 K at three, 1.24 at one.

The conclusion is uncomfortable, and better said out loud than buried. The transport that closes the heterogeneity is carried by the big events of the filling phase — and those are the same events that tear the plume. They are not two separate things you can pull apart. So the recorded flux is reported here as it behaves, with the cost visible in the figure, and not as something ready to use.

The ceiling does buy one thing. Used in the inflow window it improves the centre of gravity a lot without hurting the end profile, 0.101 to 0.076 mm. So the defect and the height of the heat are linked.

18.6 — Two things that turned out cheaper than expected

Both of these remove an objection rather than answer a question, which is why they are worth keeping.

The channel does not need every frame. Read only every fifteenth recorded frame and hold it in between, and the end profile goes from 0.90 to 1.04 K. Every sixtieth — ten frames instead of six hundred — gives 1.32 K. What the replay needs from that channel changes far more slowly than the interval it was recorded at. So the worry that every new database channel costs another three quarters of a gigabyte does not hold here: it could be a fortieth of that.

And the velocity does not have to be read at all. Swap it for a free-convection scaling on the replay's own anomaly, $F = \rho c_p A |\theta|^{3/2}$, gated to the statically unstable bands, and the heterogeneity still reaches 0.67 K — one constant, no preparation run. It is not carried further here, because on the other three measures it stays well behind the recorded channel and its plume is stepped in the same way. It is reported because of what it says about the recording: a single scaling law recovers most of the structural gain, and the rest is what the measurement adds on top.
