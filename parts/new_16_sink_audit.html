16 — What the Reference Actually Removes

Every number on this page that says how much of the CFD cooling the replay captures is a ratio, and the bottom of that ratio is the cooling of the reference. That bottom had been taken on trust. It is worth measuring, because the replay copies the reference's own sink formula, so the two ought to be removing the same heat from the same cells.

They are not, and the difference is large enough to change how everything above should be read.

16.1 — Measuring the lid term

The heat-loss case and the adiabatic case share a mesh, an inlet schedule and a mass flow. They differ in one thing, the sink at the lid. Their fields are stored every 15 s, so each term of the heat balance can be read straight out of them: what is stored in the tank, what enters at the inlet, what leaves at the outlet, and what the lid takes.

Reading both cases and subtracting one balance from the other is better than reading either alone, because whatever bias the flux estimates carry is the same on both sides and cancels. The two inlet integrals agree to 1.3 J out of 25.3 kJ, which is the check that the pair really is a controlled one. What is left after the subtraction is the heat the lid took: 6581 J.

The formula the case declares gives a different answer. Evaluated on the reference's own fields, $h A (T_\text{cell} - T_\infty)$ with $h = 100$ W/m²K over the 1125 lid cells integrates to 1575 J over the same window. The reference removes 4.18 times the heat its stated boundary condition accounts for.

Three further routes agree. The global balance of the heat-loss case on its own asks for 6763 J. A control volume drawn around the lid cell row, fed by advection and conduction from below, asks for 8586 J. And the replay, applying the declared formula to its own lid row, removes 1930 J. The replay is faithful to the boundary condition it was given. It is the reference that is not.

16.2 — Why the gap looked like a limit

This changes how the earlier sections read. A replay removing 1930 J was being scored against a reference removing 6581 J, so most of the distance between them was never a transport problem. It was a boundary condition the two sides did not share.

The shape of the error says the same. In the plain replay the upper layer ended 7.8 K too warm, and it was too warm almost uniformly: the difference map at the end of the run showed the whole region above the thermocline in one flat colour, at a mean absolute difference of 5.20 K. That is a level error, not a shape error. A sharpening step moves heat between neighbouring cells and changes the shape of a front. It cannot lower the temperature of a whole well-mixed layer, because inside that layer there is nowhere for the heat to go. That is not a matter of strength: the step is conservative by construction, taking the same amount from one cell that it gives to its neighbour, so its net effect on the heat in the tank is exactly zero. Only the wall term and the outlet can change that total.

This also explains the shape of the earlier conclusions. The capture ratio and the mean temperature are both dominated by the missing 4.7 kJ, which is the same in every run of the family. What varied between the operators was the small change each made to how much heat reached the outlet, and against a constant deficit that variation was the only thing the metric could see. So every form of the step appeared to cost cooling, and the family appeared to meet a ceiling. Both readings are correct as observations and wrong as explanations. So every form of the step was tried against an error it had no way of reaching, and the family looked as though it had met a ceiling.

The montages carried the same signature and were read the same way. Frame after frame showed a warm upper layer that no operator would shift, and the natural reading was that the recorded motion did not carry enough heat downward. Part of that reading survives, and it is the front. The larger part does not.

One earlier remark needs correcting in the light of this. Section 13 turned $h$ up and called the result papering over the missing flow with an unphysical coefficient. On the evidence above, raising the replay's coefficient is not papering over anything: it is setting the replay to remove the heat the reference actually removes. The physical objection was reasonable when the reference's own lid term had not been measured. It no longer holds.

16.3 — What the extra removal is not

The number invites an obvious question: where does the extra heat go? The source term was instrumented so it could answer for itself. It records the value it hands the solver into a user memory, and a hook sums that over the field at the end of every time step. Over a window inside the hot plateau it reports 3.44 W over exactly 1125 cells. That is the declared formula, over the intended cells. The source term is not saying one thing and doing another.

Six further candidates were then run rather than argued about, each as a short window restarted from the same stored field.

The method is sound, and the imbalance appears only when the sink is switched on. With the density held constant, so that the cooled lid can no longer drive any overturning, the imbalance largely goes with it.

The extra removal is therefore tied to the buoyancy-driven convection the sink sets off, and not to the sink's own value, its linearisation, the time step or the iteration count.

16.4 — The same coefficient as a boundary condition

There is one more way to ask the question, and it is the most direct. The lid term does not have to be a volumetric source at all. The same $h$ and the same ambient temperature can be handed to the solver as a convective wall boundary condition, which is how such a term is usually written. If the removal is a property of cooling the lid at $h = 100$ W m−2K−1, the two routes should agree.

The enclosure is a single wall zone of 16 700 faces, so the condition cannot simply be set on it — that would cool every wall. A profile hands $h$ to the faces at the top of the tank and zero to the rest. The volumetric source is switched off in that build, and the case, the restart field, the window and the time step are unchanged.

The two selections are not quite the same size, and it is worth saying so before the numbers are read. The source selects cells, and the top row is exactly 1125 of them. The profile selects wall faces by height, and the test it was first given, $y > y_\text{max} - 0.5$ mm with $y_\text{max} = 74.5$ mm the highest cell centre, also caught the topmost row of faces on the side walls, whose centres sit at 74.5 mm. That is 180 extra faces, 16 % more area than the lid alone. The measurement below therefore reads high by up to that amount, which widens the gap it reports rather than narrowing it: 4.5 W becomes about 3.9 W for the lid on its own, against 13.2 W for the volumetric path. The test has since been written as $y > y_\text{max} + 0.2$ mm, which leaves the lid alone.

The wall condition removes 4.48 W, close to the 3.44 W the declared formula gives on the reference's own field. It reads a little above it for a plain reason: with no source cooling the top row, the fluid there stays warmer, so the same coefficient sees a larger temperature difference. What matters is the scale — about four and a half watts where the volumetric path removes about thirteen.

So the extra removal follows the volumetric path and not the physical statement of the boundary condition. That is as far as this evidence reaches: it says where to look, not what the mechanism is. The window is short and starts from a field already cooled for 180 s, so the absolute watts would move over a full run and the ratio is what should be read. The number is reported as measured, and every comparison below is made against the heat the reference actually removes.

16.5 — How each one is implemented

Both routes are a few lines, and they are worth putting side by side, because the whole of this section rests on the difference between them. Both need the same thing first: the height of the top of the tank. An on-demand function walks the cells once before the run and reduces the largest cell-centre height over the partitions, which on this mesh is $y_\text{max} = 74.5$ mm.

The volumetric source, as the reference case is run. Fluent calls it for every cell of the fluid zone:

DEFINE_SOURCE(top_heat_loss, c, t, dS, eqn)
{
    C_CENTROID(x, c, t);
    cs = pow(C_VOLUME(c, t), 1.0/3.0);        /* cell edge length */

    if(Top_region_initialized && (x[1] > (Top_y_max - cs))){

        source  = -H_EXT * (C_T(c, t) - T_AMB) / cs;   /* (W/m3) */
        dS[eqn] = -H_EXT / cs;
        return source;
    }
    dS[eqn] = 0.0;
    return 0.0;
}

The test $x_1 > y_\text{max} - c_s$ keeps the row whose centres are at 74.5 mm and drops the next row down at 73.5 mm, so the source acts on exactly 1125 cells. Dividing by $c_s$ turns the surface coefficient into a volumetric one: integrated over a cell of edge $c_s$ it is $h\,c_s^2\,(T-T_\text{amb})$, the convective loss across the cell's top face. dS is the derivative Fluent uses to linearise the term, and setting it to zero changes the imbalance by 0.1 W, which is one of the controls above.

The convective wall condition. The profile is attached to the wall zone from the journal, and Fluent calls it for every face of that zone:

DEFINE_PROFILE(lid_h_coeff, t, i_var)
{
    begin_f_loop(f, t){

        F_CENTROID(x, f, t);

        F_PROFILE(f, t, i_var) =
            (Top_region_initialized && (x[1] > (Top_y_max + 2.0e-4))) ? H_EXT : 0.0;

    }end_f_loop(f, t)
}

Faces that receive zero are adiabatic, so the floor, the side walls and the pipe walls carry no flux in either build. The threshold decides how much of the top is cooled: above $y_\text{max}$ it is the lid, 1125 faces; below it, the topmost band of the side walls comes with it.

The difference that matters is not the count. It is where the heat leaves from. The source takes it out of the cell, and nothing crosses the lid: the cell's own temperature sets the rate and there is no wall between the fluid and the ambient. The condition takes it out through the face, so the heat has to conduct through half a cell first and the wall resistance $\Delta y / 2k$ adds to the film resistance $1/h$. On this mesh that second resistance is about eight per cent of the total, which is small; the factor of two the runs show is not accounted for by it, and that is the open part of the finding.

16.6 — The same question over the whole run

A 25 s window is a narrow place to settle something this consequential, so the wall condition was run to the end: the same case, the same start field, 630 episodes of five steps at $\Delta t = 0.2$ s, the same 30 inner iterations, the same 1125 lid faces. The replay's own number does not move. Under the coefficient the case declares it cools 3.59 K below its adiabatic run, and it did so before any of this was measured. The other half of that statement belongs in the same paragraph. The working point of §17 is tied to the reference as it is run. Against the wall-condition case it removes 155 % of the cooling and ends 4.5 K too cold. The coefficient of 435 W m−2K−1 was set to match a removal that the volumetric path produces and the stated condition does not, so if the reference is ever re-based on the stated condition, that number goes with it.

What the run cannot settle is the field. The database was recorded from the CFD with the volumetric source, so the motion it carries belongs to the stronger cooling. Comparing fields rather than energy against the wall-condition case would need a database recorded from that case, which is a preparation run of about fourteen hours. Nothing on this page needs it; it is the natural next step if the reference is to be re-based.

16.7 — The sign of the wall term

One reading of the wall term deserves its own test, because if it held it would overturn all of the above. The term is written here with the heat that leaves as the positive quantity: the cell is cooled by $-q$, and the balance target is lowered by the same amount. It can also be written as a signed source, in which $q$ is what the cell receives and is therefore negative above ambient. The second convention is the more common one, so the question is whether the result depends on the choice.

It does not. Rewriting the block in the signed convention, with every downstream sign following, reproduces the original run byte for byte across all 601 samples. The convention is a way of writing it down, not a physical choice.

The mistake that the convention invites is a different matter, and it was run as well. If the field is cooled while the balance target is raised by the same amount, the global-balance correction has to put the heat back. That run ends at 307.93 K against 303.81 K for the code as it stands: +4.12 K, and warmer than a run with no sink at all. The cooling disappears completely. The replay does the opposite, ending 2.5 K below its own adiabatic run, which is the direct evidence that it is not making that slip.
