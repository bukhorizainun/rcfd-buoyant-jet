17 — The Operator Family on a Matched Sink

With both sides removing the same heat, the operators can be compared on their own merits. The runs below all use a lid coefficient set so that the replay removes 6623 J against the reference's 6581 J. Nothing else about the database, the schedule or the limiter changes.

Two labels are used from here on, and it is worth fixing what they mean before the tables use them.

Declared sink is the replay run exactly as the case specifies: the wall coefficient set to the $h = 100$ W m−2K−1 the case declares, with no operator. It is faithful to the written boundary condition, it removes 1930 J, and it is where the earlier sections were working. It is kept in every table as the column to improve on, because it shows what the replay does when it is told only what the case says.

Working point is the configuration this section arrives at: the coefficient raised so that the replay removes the heat the reference actually removes, plus the operator and strength that come out best on the ladder. The two differ in one quantity that was measured rather than chosen, the sink level, and in one that was tuned, the operator strength. Everything else, including the database and the schedule, is the same in both.

Setting the sink alone, with no operator at all, already moves most of the distance. The end profile falls from an RMS departure of 5.15 K to 2.26 K, the mean temperature from 4.11 to 1.10 K over the run, and the top of the tank from 7.8 K too warm to 3.0 K too cold. Whatever is left after that is what the local rules have to work on, and it is a different problem from the one the earlier sections were solving.

17.1 — One change that was hiding the rest

The first result had nothing to do with which operator is used. It is when the operator is allowed to run.

The earlier work split the replay into two windows and stopped the sharpening when the inflow stopped, at 315 s. That choice was made for good reasons at the time: run from the start and left running, the step was seen to interfere with the rising jet. On a matched sink the split costs most of what the operator can give. The directional operator with the vertical normal, at the same strength, reaches an end-profile RMS of 2.18 K when it stops at 315 s and 0.42 K when it is left running for the whole replay.

The reason is visible in the cooling phase. After the inflow stops, the lid keeps working and the thermocline keeps being eroded by the film it produces. That is exactly when a sharpening step has something to hold, and the split schedule switched it off at that moment. On the earlier, weaker sink there was little film to speak of, so the loss was small and the split looked harmless.

Three further switches inside the family were tried on the matched sink and none of them helps: restricting the operator to a band under the lid gives 2.27 K, gating it on the strength of the local gradient 1.74 K, smoothing the gradient normal over neighbours 1.03 K, and blending the gradient normal with the vertical one 0.45 K.

17.3 — Letting the database set the strength

There is one more variant, and it fits the rest of the study better than a tuned constant does. The strength need not be a number chosen by hand. The database already stores, frame by frame and cell by cell, the sub-grid mixing the CFD actually had. Where that recorded diffusivity is large, the broad front the replay carries is largely physical and should be left alone. Where it is small, the width is numerical, and that is where an up-gradient step belongs. Writing the weight as

$w \;=\; 1 - D_\text{face} / D_\text{ref}$

with $D_\text{ref}$ the largest recorded value in the field at that step, taken by reduction, the strength follows the recording instead of a constant. Nothing is tuned by hand except the overall level.

Its ladder peaks at $s = 0.5$ with an end-profile RMS of 0.36 K, level with the best fixed-strength operator.

Two configurations are carried forward rather than one, because the numbers cannot separate them. The directional operator with the vertical normal and the stability test, at $s = 0.475$, is the plain minimum of the ladder at 0.34 K, and it is the simpler of the two to state: a fixed direction, a fixed strength, one test. The database-set strength reaches 0.36 K, the same answer within the resolution of the metric, and it reaches it with the strength following the recorded diffusivity instead of a number chosen by hand. The fixed one is the smaller claim; the database-set one is the same idea the rest of the study rests on, that the recording rather than a global constant should decide what the replay does. Where a figure below shows a single field, it is the database-set strength, because that is the run whose frames were rendered.

17.5 — What is left

One defect survives the matched sink, and it shows up in two places at once. The heterogeneity lags through the whole heating phase: the reference rises to 15.6 K by 100 s and holds, while the replay climbs more slowly and reaches 14.1 K by 310 s, running 1.5 to 4 K low throughout. The same thing appears in the difference maps as a thin line at the thermocline while the inflow is hot. After the inflow stops, both close and stay closed.

So the front builds too slowly and too softly while the tank is filling, and everything else follows. That is a smaller and much better posed question than the one this study began with, and it is where the next round belongs.

One detail in the clips above is worth naming, because it answers a doubt that ran through the earlier sections. Cold fluid now falls from the lid in the replay, and it did not before. Nothing was added to make it: the replay cannot invent motion, it only follows the recorded cell-to-cell map, and that map was recorded from a CFD whose lid was already cooled. The descending plumes were in the database all along. What was missing was something for them to carry. Under the declared coefficient the sink pulled 1930 J, too little to build a film dense enough to show, so the same motion moved fluid that barely differed from its surroundings. With the sink matched the film forms and the recorded motion takes it down. The database was never the problem.
