12 — rCFD vs CFD: the Gap

One check before the comparison. With the loss switched off ($h = 0$), the replay gives back the Step 1 result exactly: $T_\text{mean}$ and $CoG_y$ match the no-loss run with no difference, and $Q_\text{wall}$ stays at zero the whole time. So any cooling we see below comes only from the heat-loss term, not from a side effect of the code. The balance accounting that carries that term is checked separately in the $h$-sweep (§13), where it closes to about $0.03\%$.

Now the cheap side. We kept the existing adiabatic C2C database from Step 1 (no rebuild) and simply added the heat-loss source term to the replay. Because the absolute rCFD temperature already carries a small replay bias from Step 1, the fair thing to compare is the cooling itself, i.e. how much each method drops when heat loss is turned on:

The key Step-2 result

The reused adiabatic database captures only about a third of the real heat loss: at $h = 100$ rCFD cools by 3.03 K against the CFD's 9.79 K (a ratio of ≈ 0.31), both read at $t = 600$ s. The same ordering shows up in $\delta T_\text{mean}$, where rCFD picks up only about a third of the CFD change. (At the gentle physical $h = 8$ the gap is even wider, rCFD 0.36 K against CFD 1.45 K, a ratio of ≈ 0.25, but the cooling there is so small it is hard to see; turning $h$ up makes the shortfall plain.)

Why does it fall so far short, with roughly two-thirds of the heat loss missing? Because we reused the adiabatic database from Step 1, the fluid flow in the replay is completely frozen: it only knows the motion that was recorded with the lid closed. In the real CFD, once the water at the top cools it gets heavier and sinks, and warm water rises to take its place. This is the convective overturning loop from Figure 8, and it keeps feeding warm fluid to the surface, so the heat loss keeps going. The replay does not contain that loop, because nothing was cooling when the database was recorded. So in the replay the top water cools in place: the surface difference $T_\text{top} - T_\infty$ shrinks, and the loss slows down. The missing piece is a flow pattern, not a diffusion setting, so we call it an advection gap. It fits how rCFD splits transport in the first place: the recorded shifts carry the advection, while diffusion only adds the small-scale mixing on top [5]. The Step-1 replay bias (~1.7 K on the absolute level) accounts for only a small part of the gap; the frozen flow is the main cause. This is the limit of reusing an adiabatic database and adding a source term on top, and it is the result Step 2 was meant to show. It is also a known soft spot of the method: applied to heat transfer in a fluidised bed, transport-based rCFD smeared out the sharp, localised heat-transfer layer that the flow drives [2]. Our under-captured cold cap is the buoyant-jet version of the same difficulty, reproducing a localised, flow-driven thermal structure on a frozen flow.

What the gap really measures

It helps to read the shortfall as a test of database reuse, not just a heat-loss result. The recurrence database was recorded once, from the adiabatic flow, and Step 2 reuses it under a boundary condition it never saw. The roughly two-thirds shortfall at $h = 100$ (wider at the gentle $h = 8$) is then a direct measure of how far that one database stretches: the physics it did record (the advection in the shift maps) still carries over, while the physics it never recorded (the cooling-driven down-flow) cannot appear no matter how the source term is tuned. So the number to keep from Step 2 is not the cooling itself but how much of it a reused adiabatic database can recover, which §15 takes up by rebuilding the database from a heat-loss run.
