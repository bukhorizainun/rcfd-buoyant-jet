15 — Rebuilding the Database with Heat Loss

Section 12 pinned the gap on a missing flow pattern: the adiabatic database never saw the cooling-driven down-flow, so the replay cannot make it. The obvious fix is to build the database from a run that already has the heat loss in it, so those down-flows are recorded from the start. We did that. We re-ran the CFD with the $h = 100$ wall loss switched on, recorded a fresh 600-frame C2C database from it (the non-adiabatic database), and replayed the same heat-loss case on it. Everything else was held fixed: $f_{sd} = 0.5$, $h = 100$, read at $t = 600$ s.

The result is the surprise of Step 2. The rebuilt database does not close the gap. Measured the fair way, the cooling each method reaches from its own no-loss baseline, it does a little worse:

The reason is in how the non-adiabatic run settles. With the loss on, the jet runs colder, so it is less buoyant, so its circulation is weaker. The database records that weaker loop. A weaker loop carries less warm fluid up to the surface where the loss acts, so the frozen flow, even though it was born under cooling, moves less heat to where it can leave. The deeper point is that the real cooling is a transient. The flow starts hot and vigorous and slows as the tank cools, passing through a whole sequence of regimes. A single database freezes one of them: the adiabatic one freezes the hot, vigorous end, the non-adiabatic one freezes the cold, weak end. Neither holds the sequence, and the sequence is what the heat loss needs.

One thing the rebuild does improve is the evenness of the field. $\delta T_\text{mean}$ drops from $10.78$ K on the adiabatic database to $9.05$ K, a step toward the CFD's $7.32$ K, because the recorded cooled flow is already better mixed. Pushing the face-swap up to $f_{sd} = 0.75$ takes it a little further, to $\delta T_\text{mean} = 8.36$ K and $T_\text{mean} = 303.01$ K. But that is a sensitivity check, not a fix: more face-swap is more diffusion, and the gap is advective, so the cooling saturates well short of the CFD, the same lesson as the $h$-sweep in §13. We keep $f_{sd} = 0.5$ as the value to report, the one anchored by the local-diffusion field in §9.3.

15.1 — How the heat-loss database is built

Only one thing changes between the two databases, and it is where the heat loss acts. For the adiabatic database (Step 1), the prep CFD is sealed: it records the buoyant flow with no loss, and the wall loss is added only later, at replay, as the passive scalar sink from §10.1. For the non-adiabatic database, the same loss is switched on inside the prep CFD itself. It is the CFD-side source term from §10.1, a volumetric sink in the top cell layer, $\dot q = -h\,(T - T_\infty)/\Delta x$ (W/m³), added to the energy equation rather than to a passive scalar. So the cooling is felt by the real temperature, which changes the density, which changes the buoyancy, which changes the flow. The recorder then writes that cooled flow into the C2C maps. Everything else is identical to Step 1: same mesh, same 600 frames, same recurrence settings, only the recorded flow now carries the cooling-driven down-flow.

At replay the heat loss is applied once more, the same passive scalar sink used on the adiabatic database, so the table above is a like-for-like test: both runs drive the temperature scalar with the identical $h = 100$ term, and the only difference is whether the recorded flow underneath it ever felt the cooling. That is what makes the result clean to read — the 26%-against-31% difference is down to the flow database alone, not to the heat-loss term.

15.3 — Rebuilding it once more, with the local-diffusivity field on board

15.4 — Sharpening on the recorded cooling flow

The negative-diffusivity operators come over unchanged, and they behave exactly as they did on the adiabatic database. Every sharpening form reduces the captured cooling, the vertical normal stays ahead of the gradient-based one, and the sensor-adaptive strength lands on its fixed counterpart. The mechanism survives the rebuild intact. The wall term feeds on a warm top layer. Smearing keeps refilling that layer, so the flux stays alive. Sharpening locks a thin cold film on top, and the flux starves. A recorded cooling flow does not unseal the film, because its plumes sit at the recorded times and places, not where the replay's own film forms.

This ranking is surprising, and it is worth pausing on. A weight that takes its direction from the data ought to be the smarter of the two. Where the front is flat, it finds the vertical direction on its own, with nothing set by hand. The problem is that it reads all of the data, not just the part that matters. The vertical normal carries one piece of prior knowledge: in a stratified tank, the front that matters is the horizontal one gravity builds. That knowledge acts as a filter. The sideways ripples the replay's transport keeps producing get no sharpening, because their gradients point the wrong way. The gradient weight has no such filter. Every ripple is a front to it, so it steepens the noise with the same care as the signal. And once it has steepened a spurious intrusion, the intrusion's own gradient keeps the weight pointed at it, a feedback that fixes the artefact in place. The green band inside the hot layer in Figure 17 is that feedback made visible. Under heat loss the same trait works directly against the capture. The sharpest structure in the field is the cold film itself, so the data-driven weight seals it more completely than the vertical form does. The prescribed normal wins not because it knows more physics, but because it ignores more noise.

A stability guard: how to stop the sharpening from hurting the cooling. The cooling is lost because the operator sharpens the one front it should leave alone: the cold film, cold sitting above warm. The fix is to teach it the difference. The bounded step already carries one guard, the head-room limiter that keeps the exchange inside the neighbour range, so it can never overshoot (this is the flux-corrected-transport rule, Boris & Book 1973, Zalesak 1979, Kuzmin & Turek 2002). That limiter controls the amplitude of the step. What the heat-loss case needs is a guard on its direction, and the physical criterion for it is static stability. A vertical pair with the colder cell on top is statically unstable, the classic configuration gravity wants to overturn (Turner 1973; Chandrasekhar 1961). Sharpening across it drives heat further from the surface, the wrong way for a wall that cools from above. So we add one test to the face loop: on a near-vertical link whose upper cell is the colder one, skip the sharpening. Everywhere else, on the stable main thermocline and every horizontal link, the operator runs unchanged. The guard is ours, but it sits in the same family as the limiter it extends. Both refuse the anti-diffusive step where it would do harm, one by amplitude and the other by orientation.

The guarded rows show the effect, and it is in the right direction. The guard recovers part of the lost cooling and tightens the field at once. It helps most where the operator had been doing most harm: the isotropic form climbs from $7\%$ to $14\%$, the gradient form from $16\%$ to $17\%$, and the vertical form from $22\%$ to $24\%$. The guarded vertical run has the smallest trace deviation of the whole sharpening family ($3.43$ K), because the sharp main front is kept while the film is left to breathe. The guarded gradient run is the telling one. The guard heals that form's centreline artefact: the end-profile width returns from 46 mm to 5.6 mm. But it buys the least cooling of the three, because sharpening its own ripples, the other half of its penalty, is invisible to a stability test. And the guard stops at the plain replay, which is the honest ceiling. The remaining shortfall is not from the film, which is now skipped. It is from the main thermocline the operator is still, correctly, sharpening. Steepening a stable front also slows the slow vertical spread of heat across it, and that spread was part of what fed the surface. A sharpening step can be kept from hurting the cooling. It cannot be made to add cooling, because adding cooling means carrying heat to the surface, and that is a different job.

What the sharpening cannot do is add cooling, because adding cooling means carrying heat to the surface, and that is transport rather than shaping. Two ways of supplying it, a penetrative mixing profile and a prescribed circulation, are a study of their own and are kept together in §15.11. The wall-side account that explains why they work sits in §15.8.

15.5 — Turning the same test around, and giving each operator its own window

The guard of §15.4 has to recognise a statically unstable pair before it can skip it, so the operator already knows where the field is unstable. Under heat loss those pairs are not incidental: the sink manufactures them, cooling the top cell layer so that cold sits above warm beneath the whole lid. In the reference that state does not survive, because the film breaks into plumes that sink while warm fluid rises to replace them. The replay cannot do this on its own. But the same local test can be given the opposite instruction: where a near-vertical pair is unstable, exchange heat down the gradient until the pair is neutral. Nothing new is measured, and no length scale is introduced. The direction still comes from the local gradient, and static stability decides the sign. One form mixes the pair towards its mass-weighted mean, the classical convective adjustment; the other swaps the two cells outright, so a cold parcel falls one cell per sweep undiluted, closer to what a plume does.

It works, and it is the first local step in this study to raise the captured cooling rather than trade it: $43.0\%$ for the mixing form and $45.0\%$ for the swapping one, against $35.2\%$ for the plain replay, with a better mean-temperature trace in both cases ($3.0$ K against $3.64$ K).

When the rule is allowed to act matters more than how hard it acts. The frames exposed a defect the numbers hid. While the hot inflow is still filling the tank, the rule tears the rising plume into vertical filaments, so a single inlet appears to produce three jets. The cause is in the test: a plume climbing through colder water carries warm fluid under cold fluid at its leading edge, which is a statically unstable column by the criterion the rule uses. The cold film the rule was written for does not exist yet at that stage, so the rule is held until the inflow stops. The gate can be checked instead of trusted: before the switch a gated run reproduces the plain replay to $0.003$ K, against $2.06$ K for the ungated one, which is the tearing measured. Holding it back also pays, and the capture rises to $47.1\%$ with ten sweeps and $47.3\%$ with thirty.

Each operator in its own window. The sharpening builds the front while the jet runs, and it is also what blocks the transport once the cooling starts; the pair rule is the reverse. So the sharpening is switched off when the inflow stops and the pair rule switched on there. That combination is the best of the whole local family on two counts at once: $47.8\%$ of the CFD cooling, the highest here, and a mean-temperature deviation of $2.80$ K, the smallest here. The control run says how much belongs to the sharpening: the same schedule with no sharpening reproduces the gated pair rule exactly, $47.1\%$, so the sharpening window is worth $0.7$ points of capture and $0.38$ K of trace. It earns its place for the shape it leaves behind. The end state pays for it in one column, the front spreading back to $9.4$ mm against $5.0$ mm for a run sharpened throughout, with $4.5$ mm in the reference.

15.6 — The pictures against the numbers, again

§15.2 ended by warning that the animation and the metric can disagree. The new runs sharpen that warning into something usable. Now there are replays that match the number, replays that match the look, and one that tries to do both.

The physics behind that last sentence is the honest limit of the whole exercise. The CFD cools through a feedback loop: the film chills, turns heavy locally, breaks into a plume, and the plume renews the surface behind it. The loop repeats, intermittent and plume-sized. Every replay in the table reaches its end state by some other route, whether smearing, sharpening, or a steady prescribed roll. The one thing a recorded or prescribed motion cannot do is respond to the temperature field the replay is creating. The transport runs close the amount, and even the end-state look. What stays different is the texture of the cooling in between. That residue is the feedback itself, and it is the cleanest statement so far of what the frozen flow leaves out.

15.7 — Measuring the texture, not just the shade

Everything in §15.6 comes from watching frames, so it is fair to ask whether a measurement says the same thing. It does, and no new run is needed. Every frame on this page, CFD and replay alike, is rendered with the same fixed colour scale, 293–333 K, and the same view. That makes the frames comparable field by field. For each frame we cut out the chamber, map the colour back to a temperature value through its hue, and resample the chamber onto a common grid. Then we read three numbers over the cooling window, from $t = 320$ s to the end.

The first is how much structure sits off the layering: the spread of the field once each row's own mean is removed. A quiet, well-layered tank sits near zero, while plumes, rolls and blotches all raise it. The second is the one that matters most here. Compare a field's pattern with itself some seconds later, and see how fast the resemblance fades. Short-lived plumes appear and die, so the pattern renews and the resemblance drops quickly. A prescribed circulation prints the same cells frame after frame, so the resemblance holds. We report the lag at which half of it is left, and call it the renewal time. The third is the roughness of the front: how much the thermocline height varies from side to side, which a single centreline profile cannot show.

On the adiabatic database the plain replay hardly renews at all, about 116 s, which is what a frozen adiabatic recording should give. Counted in captured cooling, that rebuild was worth six points, §15.3. Counted this way, it covers most of the distance to the reference.

The transport runs move the other way. The prescribed roll doubles to quintuples the off-layering structure, leaves the renewal time between 84 s and beyond the window, and roughens the front by six to thirteen times. The sharpening runs sit in between, and the vertical form matches the CFD's front roughness most closely, which is what the mid-run frames already suggested.

So the two readings deserve to stand side by side. Ranked by captured cooling the runs go plain, mixing profile, roll. Ranked by process they go the other way, with the plain replay on the rebuilt database closest to the CFD and the roll runs furthest from it. One recovery number cannot rank the methods on its own. Two limits are worth stating: the values come from rendered frames, so they carry some compression noise, and the CFD frames are sampled from a smaller pixel box than the replay frames. Any bias from that would flatter the CFD's fine structure, and the CFD still shows the least of it, so the comparison runs against the bias rather than with it. The renewal time is built from correlations and does not depend on the amplitude scale at all.

15.8 — Where the cooling actually comes from

Everything above reads as a ladder of captured cooling, and it invites one assumption: that a run higher on the ladder must be pulling more heat through the cooled surface. That assumption is wrong, and checking it changed which operator was worth building next.

The tank is not closed. The inlet and the outlet both sit at mid height, so the mean temperature can fall for two different reasons: the wall takes heat out, or warm fluid leaves through the outlet. Both are measurable. The mass stays constant, so $\Delta H = m\,c_p\,\Delta T_\text{mean}$ with $m = 85.1$ g, taken from the CFD reference's own enthalpy and temperature traces. The wall term is the sink power the replay already writes out. What is left is the export.

So the wall side was never the problem. The replays extract 1.7–2.0 kJ over the run and the CFD extracts about 1.5–1.8 kJ. Read this together with §16.1: the figure for the CFD here is its own formula evaluated on its own fields, and the balance route gives four times as much for the same term. Which of the two is the lid term of a closed budget is not settled on this page. The CFD never wrote its sink power out, so that figure was measured from its frames: the sink depends only on the top-layer temperature, the colour scale is fixed, and the same estimate applied to a replay reproduces its known sink energy to within 8–20%. Nothing in this study needed to improve the wall term, because it already matched.

If the export is what has to change, which property of an operator changes it? Four levers were swept and only one mattered. How far a parcel travels does nothing: the sorting adjustment at ten, thirty and fifty sweeps gives the same 2248 J of wall energy and the same end temperature to two decimals. How strong the operator is at one place saturates: an entrainment exchange placed at the layer base, with its amount fixed by the surface heat loss, gives 36% and stays there even when multiplied by twenty-five, because past a point the pair at the base is simply mixed out. How deep a weak profile reaches buys three points for a five-fold change in depth. What moves the number is how much of the column is stirred at full strength: 41% at 35 mm, 43% at 50 mm, 44% at 75 mm, 52% on the recorded cooling flow, and 69–74% for a circulation that stirs the whole tank.

That restates an earlier reading of this study. The bound we had put at roughly a third of the CFD cooling belongs to operators confined near the surface; given the whole column at full strength a local rule reaches 52%. The bound is about extent, not about being local.

Two operators are new here and both are worth keeping for what they rule out. The mixed layer sets its own depth from the heat already removed, $h=\sqrt{2(E/A)/(\rho c_p \Gamma)}$, with the stratification $\Gamma$ measured in the field every step, so nothing in it is tuned. It reaches 36% and 43% on the two databases, and its depth comes out at 20–25 mm, close to the value that had been set by hand. The entrainment form keeps that depth but puts the exchange at the layer base, where the measurement said the transport belongs, and it changes almost nothing. Both are silent in an adiabatic replay by construction: with the sink off no heat accumulates, the layer stays at its floor, and the end temperature is 307.62 K, the plain adiabatic value to the last digit.

This is the honest close of the transport line. The operators that reach the CFD's cooling stir fluid the buoyancy forcing cannot justify stirring, since the cold lower half is stably stratified and nothing asks for strong mixing there. The operators whose magnitude is set by the forcing itself move too little heat to matter. Between those two sentences lies the reason the replay cannot be made to match this case at the hook: the export the CFD achieves comes from a flow that rearranges the whole tank in response to its own temperature field, and a recorded flow cannot be asked to do that. What does help, without a single new constant, is recording the right flow. Every operator gains six to eight points when the database comes from the cooling run instead of the adiabatic one.

15.10 — Letting the database place the sharpening

Every table so far judges the sharpening by the cooling it costs. That is the wrong yardstick for what the operator is meant to do, and once the field measures are read instead, two things change.

First, the database can say where to sharpen. The stored local diffusivity is the sub-grid mixing the CFD actually had at that cell and frame, and it is what the face swap applies: the volume a face swaps is $D\,A/d\,\Delta t$, which on this cubic mesh is $D\,c_s\,\Delta t$. So where the recorded value is large, part of the width the replay carries is physical and the up-gradient step should hold back; where it is small, the width is numerical and the step belongs there. The strength is therefore scaled by $w = 1 - D_\text{face}/D_\text{ref}$, with $D_\text{ref}$ the largest recorded value in the field that step, taken by reduction, so nothing in the weight is set by hand. Reading that field during a replay needed a small read-only addition to the library: the existing reader is limited to preparation mode and to frame 0, while a replay needs the state and frame the swap itself is using.

Second, the step can be gated to the front. The $\cos^2\theta$ weight says which way to move heat but not where, so a weak ripple in the well-mixed upper layer whose gradient happens to point the right way is sharpened as hard as the thermocline. Scaling by the squared face gradient against the largest gradient in the field removes that, and the exponent $p$ in $(|\nabla T|/|\nabla T|_\text{max})^p$ decides how much of the front's flanks the step still reaches.

Where this leaves the heat-loss case

The wall term looked matched from the start: the replays extract 1.7–2.0 kJ against about 1.5–1.8 kJ in the reference, that second figure being the case's own formula on its own fields (see §16.1, where the balance route gives four times as much for the same term). What is missing is the advective export, and that is produced in the CFD by a flow which rearranges the whole tank in response to its own temperature field. A recorded flow cannot be asked to do that, so the hook can only imitate it. Rebuilding the database from the cooling run is what helps without a single new constant, lifting every operator by six to eight points, and a circulation whose speed comes from the convective scale reaches 53% with nothing left to tune. Beyond that, the capture column keeps rising only when the operator stirs more of the tank than the buoyancy forcing pays for, and the field metrics show the cost.

15.11 — The base of the layer: a thickness and a position

The field measurements above all point at one place in the chamber. The sharpening operators work at the thermocline and lose on the picture; the transport operators work through the column and win on it. What both readings have in common is the base of the warm layer. It is where the export is fed from below, and it is the feature the eye reads first in a montage. So the one operator that acted there deserved a closer look than it had been given.

That operator is the step form of the encroachment closure. Inside a layer of depth $h$ below the top surface, every cell is moved toward the layer's own mass-weighted mean,

$T_i \leftarrow T_i + c\,w_i\,(\overline{T} - T_i), \qquad \overline{T} = \dfrac{\sum_j w_j V_j T_j}{\sum_j w_j V_j}$

with $c$ the share taken per step. Using the same weight $w$ in the mean and in the move is what makes it safe: the weighted mean of the increments is exactly zero, so the heat of the region is conserved whatever the weights are. That property is worth naming, because it is what the operator has instead of a physical closure. It does not carry heat between neighbours; it imposes a shape and keeps the heat of the region it acts on. A calibrated model, not a closure.

With $w_i = 1$ inside the layer and $0$ outside, that gives a base one cell thick — 2.08 mm on the end profile, against the reference's 4.48 mm. So the replay finally overshoots the reference, in the one direction the sharpening family could never reach. A real convective layer does not end in a step either. In the laboratory convection tank of Deardorff, Willis and Stockton the top of the mixed layer is not a surface but a zone, bracketed by the heights at which 5% and 90% of the fluid carries mixed-layer properties, and its thickness scales with the depth of the layer rather than being a fixed length. The form to carry over is therefore a thickness proportional to $h$, with the proportionality $\zeta$ the single number left to set:

$w_i = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\dfrac{h + \tfrac{1}{2}\zeta h - d_i}{\zeta h},\,0,\,1\right)$

The weight is one above the zone, falls linearly through it, and is zero below; the conservation argument holds unchanged, and $\zeta = 0$ recovers the step.

Reading the depth off the profile

That fixes how the layer ends but not where. The depth still came from the heat that had left through the wall, and that budget is one-way: heat only accumulates, so the layer it predicts can only deepen. The reference does something else. Its warm layer deepens to about 0.43 of the chamber height, stalls there, and rises again once the inflow changes, because cold fluid has collected underneath and a stable interface stops the convection reaching further. A depth read from the field carries that behaviour without being told about it.

Ocean mixed layers are diagnosed exactly that way. de Boyer Montégut and co-workers take the base as the shallowest depth at which the temperature departs from a near-surface reference by more than a fixed threshold, $\Delta T = 0.2$ K below the value at 10 m, and show on moored records that the criterion follows the base of the layer through the seasonal cycle. Transferred to the chamber, the temperature is averaged over horizontal bands, the reference comes from the topmost bands that hold fluid, and

$h = \min\{\,d : T_\text{ref} - \langle T\rangle(d) > \Delta T_\text{crit}\,\}$

The vertical range of this case falls by roughly a factor of three over the run, so a fixed 0.2 K would mean different things early and late; the threshold is a share of the instantaneous range of the profile instead. Nothing in it is fitted to the reference, since both the range and the reference level come from the replay. Two consequences follow. The depth no longer needs the sink, so unlike the budget form this variant is not silent in an adiabatic replay and has to be checked there separately. And the depth can now decrease, which the budget form could never do.

The first attempt failed in a way worth recording, because any profile-diagnosed closure can fall into it. Early in the run the chamber is close to isothermal, no crossing exists, and the code read that as one layer filling the whole column. The operator then mixed the whole column, which removed the very interface the test looks for, so the decision held from the first step onward and the run spent the entire cooling window fully stirred. Its endpoint looked respectable while its trajectory was nowhere near the reference: a mean-temperature deviation of 7.86 K against 2.72 K for the budget form. Reading a missing crossing as no layer instead, so the depth falls back to its floor and the operator stays quiet until a front forms, drops that deviation to 1.31 K with nothing else changed.

What the step does to the picture

At this point the monitors are as close as they have been anywhere in the study. The best of these runs follows the reference mean temperature to 0.92 K over the whole window, holds the heat centroid to 0.08 mm, and captures 81% of the cooling, all at the same time. Judged on those columns it is the working point.

The picture says something else. Two presentation faults had to be cleared before it could be trusted, and both mislead in the same direction.

The first is the crop. The earlier montages start at the left wall of the tank, which hides the two channels: the warm stream enters through a channel at mid height and the outlet leaves through another at the same height. Without them in view the interface reads as a line drawn across the picture for no reason, when it is the height at which the tank is fed and drained. The montage below reaches a third of a tank width to the left so the channel is visible, and the dividing line is then plainly the channel level. The reference's own pathline panel confirms it: the tank runs as two separated circulation cells with the channel level between them.

The second is the camera. The renderer does not always return the same view — three turned up across the runs of this study — and a crop fixed by hand then compares one tank against a differently sized tank elsewhere in the frame. That fault alone put a field distance of 10.5 K on a run whose true distance is 1.86 K. The tank is now located in every frame from the colour itself.

With that cleared, the field measurement is unambiguous, and it disagrees with the monitors. Setting every cell of the layer to the layer mean, which is what $c = 1$ does, flattens the vertical profile and every horizontal difference along with it: the warm stream that should climb from the channel to the top wall is averaged away, and the replay shows a featureless slab above a hard line. Leaving part of the grading in place, $c = 0.7$, keeps the stream and gives the closest field of the whole study.

Read with the correction below: the capture figures in this section are what the operator does, not what the surface heat budget authorises. The last nine points of capture can be bought back. Raising the threshold to $\Delta T_\text{crit} = 0.15$ at the same $c = 0.7$ gives 80.9% of the cooling with the closest mean-temperature trace of the study, 0.92 K, and a field distance of 1.93 K — still ahead of the prescribed roll at 20 mm/s, still behind the same operator at the lower threshold. The layer it leaves is a shade too cold, cyan against the reference's green, and that is where the last of the field distance sits.

That diagnosis can be tested rather than asserted. So preserving the anomaly is sound in itself; combining it with a depth read off the same profile is not.

It is not a clear win even there, and the field measurement is what says so: 2.76 against 2.55 K for the same depth with the structure erased. Preserving the anomaly buys the front and the centroid and loses a little of the field, because the departures it keeps are the ones the recorded flow carries, and those are not the reference’s departures. Keeping structure and keeping the right structure are different requirements, and only the second one closes the picture.

Where the extra cooling came from

Reading the depth off the profile takes a local rule from a third of the cooling to four fifths of it, and it would be comfortable to leave the story there. One more test says the four fifths is borrowed, and the montage had been saying so from the first minute of the run.

The tell is at the start. In the early frames the whole upper half of the tank turns into one flat block with a hard lower edge, before the sink has removed enough heat to have mixed anything at all. The reason is that the depth criterion cannot tell two similar-looking interfaces apart. While the tank is filling, the warm inflow leaves a front behind it, and that front is a stable one: nothing has stirred the fluid above it. The criterion finds it, calls it the base of a convective layer, and the operator homogenises a region the surface cooling has never touched.

The surface heat budget is the quantity that knows better, since mixing a layer of depth $h$ into a gradient costs a heat deficit the surface must actually have removed. Keeping the smaller of the two, $h = \min(h_\text{profile}, h_\text{budget})$, gives each quantity the job it is good for: the profile says where the interface is, the budget says how deep the cooling can have reached by now. Early on the budget is near zero, so the operator stays out of the way and the filling proceeds untouched.

The capture then falls from 79% to 43%, back among the runs whose depth came from the budget in the first place. So the extra cooling was not produced by reading the field better. It was produced by stirring a region the forcing does not pay for, and the flat block in the early frames was that overreach made visible. It is the same conclusion the lever sweeps reach from the opposite direction: what sets the capture is how much of the tank is stirred, and a closure that stirs more than the surface loss can justify will always look better on the capture column than it deserves.

The honest frontier

Within what the heat budget authorises, a local rule on this database gives about 45% of the cooling, a front of 5.9 mm against the reference's 4.48, and a bulk still several Kelvin too warm. The field distance of 1.24 K stands as a measurement, but it belongs to a run whose capture is borrowed in the same way, so it is the closest field reached in this study rather than a validated closure. Everything past that — here and in the prescribed-roll chapter alike — is bought by moving more fluid than the measured surface loss can account for. And what none of it supplies is the row of cold fingers hanging from the cooled lid, which is plume-scale structure and beyond a rule acting on the horizontal mean.

What the recorded diffusivity says

Two questions about the stored diffusivity had been left open, and both matter for the whole sharpening line: whether the field varies enough for the word local to mean anything, and what it says near the lid, since the cold film there drives both the wall flux and the descending plumes. The read-only accessor makes them answerable — the field is summed by depth every step and written out, with the operator left on at zero strength so the run reads the field and changes nothing.

The field is strongly non-uniform: cell values span zero to $1.27\times10^{-4}$ m²/s, so it is not a relabelled constant. The lid behaves in two regimes. Averaged over the top millimetre against the rest of the tank, the ratio is 6.0 at $t=60$ s, 3.9 at 300 s, then 0.59 at 450 s and 0.53 at the end. While the tank fills, the ceiling is the most actively mixed part of it, which is what the arriving warm stream spreading along the lid should look like; once the inflow stops, the same region mixes only about half as much as the bulk. So the database does record damped mixing at the lid through the cooling window, and a film ought to survive there.

It does not, and the reason is size rather than physics. The volume a face swaps in one step is $D\,c_s\,\Delta t$ on this cubic mesh, which against the cell volume is the diffusion number $\mathrm{Di} = D\,\Delta t / c_s^2$. With $c_s = 1$ mm and $\Delta t = 1$ s the lid value gives $\mathrm{Di} = 2.3$, the bulk value 4.4, and the largest recorded value of order $10^2$. An explicit diffusive step stops being a small perturbation above about 0.5; at 2 to 4 a cell is levelled with its neighbours within one step. The library must be limiting the exchange internally, which was not checked here, but the conclusion does not depend on the limiter: at this time step the recorded diffusivity is far outside the range in which a diffusive step preserves millimetre-scale structure. That is the quantitative root of the smearing the whole negative-diffusivity line was built to fight, and it is why no operator acting once per second could hold a one-millimetre film.

The diffusion number is linear in $\Delta t$, which names the obvious remedy; and shortening the interval between shifts is in any case an established correction when a replay reproduces the character of a case but not its magnitude, because the recorded circulation is then too slow (Zhang et al. 2025). Both readings point the same way, so it was tested on the same database with no operator at all, with the episode count raised to keep the physical window at 600 s. The capture rises from 35.2% at $\Delta t = 1$ s to 67.9% at 0.5 s and 76.0% at 0.75 s — more than any local operator here reaches within its budget.

One number doing two jobs

The diffusivity argument can be tested rather than believed, and the test is one run. For a steady flux through a layer one cell thick the drop across it is $\Delta T_\text{film} = q\,c_s / (\rho c_p D)$, so the reference's measured film of 1.54 K implies an effective diffusivity near the lid of about $2\times10^{-7}$ m²/s — essentially the molecular value for water. The database records $2.3\times10^{-6}$ there, an order of magnitude more. If that relation is the whole story, removing the face-swap diffusion should hand the film back.

It does. With the local field switched off and the swap coefficient taken down in steps, the film grows from 0.57 K to 1.17, then 1.45, then 1.49 K against the reference's 1.54. The near-wall structure the replay was missing is not missing for want of an operator; it is dissolved by the diffusivity the database carries.

What a rebuilt database would have to achieve

A finer frame interval lowers the recurrence diffusivity, because more of the motion is captured by the shift and less is left to be represented as diffusion. But it lowers it everywhere, and the table above is what happens when $D$ falls uniformly. A rebuild can only win if it moves the near-lid transport out of the diffusion term and into the shift term, so the plume motion is recorded as displacement rather than left as diffusivity. Whether a finer prep does that is an open question, not a safe assumption — but it is cheap to check before committing ten hours to it. The diffusivity probe costs one replay: if the new database shows $D$ at the lid an order of magnitude smaller while $D$ in the bulk holds, the film will survive without giving up the transport. If both fall together, the rebuild reproduces the trade above and there is no reason to continue.

The field goes the wrong way, by two independent measures. The heterogeneity at the end falls to 2.0–2.2 K against the reference's 6.76, so the tank is close to well mixed; and the topmost cell ends at 296 K against the reference's 306 K, colder than its own bulk mean, so the layering is not merely weakened but inverted. Accelerating a recorded circulation accelerates it everywhere, including through the stably stratified lower half that the real flow leaves alone. Adding the layer operator on top recovers part of the heterogeneity, 2.2 to 4.7 K, and still leaves the centroid four times further from the reference than the operator alone manages. So the time-step lever buys what every other lever here buys and pays in the same coin; it is recorded for that reason rather than as a candidate.

15.12 — The smallest feature the replay can hold

Running the same probe on the adiabatic database gives the reference point, and it also gives the study its one unifying number. In the cooling window the two databases are almost identical near the lid: $2.16\times10^{-6}$ m²/s against $2.52\times10^{-6}$, both about half their own bulk value. They differ only while the tank fills, where the cooling run carries an order of magnitude more mixing at the ceiling because the sink is already driving convection there. So the order-of-magnitude gap to the reference's near-molecular film belongs to this kind of database, not to the heat-loss case in particular.

Turning the diffusion number around gives a length instead of a criterion. A feature of size $L$ survives a step while $D\,\Delta t / L^2$ stays below about a half, so the replay has a floor

$L_\text{min} = \sqrt{2 D \Delta t}$

which for the bulk diffusivity of either database and the one-second step used in every run of this study is 2.93 mm. Nothing smaller can be maintained, whatever operator asks for it.

Molecular water would put the floor at 0.53 mm, which is why the reference can carry a film the replay cannot.

15.13 — Recording the transport, not only the flow

Everything in §15.4 to §15.12 is an operator: a rule that rearranges the field the replay already has. Each one improves a column and pays for it in another, and together they settle near half of the CFD cooling. The reason is the same one §12 gave: what is missing is transport, and no rule that only shuffles the existing field can invent it. But transport is exactly the thing a recording can hand over.

So the database was given one more channel. During preparation the CFD writes, for every frame and every cell, the vertical convective heat flux it is carrying at that moment:

$F = \rho\,c_p\,v\,\bigl(T - \langle T\rangle_\text{band}\bigr)$

with $v$ the vertical velocity and $\langle T\rangle_\text{band}$ the volume-weighted mean temperature of the horizontal band of cells at the same height. The departure is taken from the band on purpose. Warm fluid rising and cold fluid sinking at the same height cancel in any average over that height — and that cancellation is what the cell-to-cell map performs when it is built. The band anomaly is the part that survives in the CFD and disappears from the map.

At replay the file for the frame being played is read back and its divergence enters the temperature equation as a source: the vertical part of each face area carries the face value of the flux, $\Delta q = F_\text{face}\,|A_y|\,\Delta t$, from the lower cell to the upper one. Every transfer is capped by the same head-room limiter the sharpening uses, so no cell can be pushed past the range of its own neighbours. The flux comes from the reference; the boundedness does not have to be given up for it.

Checked before it was believed

A new database channel can be wrong in ways that still look plausible, so the path was tested first. The preparation is inert: the writer only reads solver fields, and its mean-temperature trace matches a preparation run made before the writer existed, digit for digit; the database it produced has the same local-diffusivity and recurrence files as the earlier one, by checksum. The frame is the right frame: against a synthetic database where every cell of frame $f$ holds the value $f$, the sum read back is $f\times$ the cell count at all 600 steps, and the frame number in the reader's log matches the library's own report of what it is replaying. Reading is free: with the flux read but multiplied by zero, the replay is bit-identical to the run without the channel. The sign is right: on a uniform upward test flux the term moves energy up and takes it out of the fluid below the lid; the energy leaving the bulk per step, divided by the imposed flux, gives 1.11 × 10−3 m² against a cooled surface of 1.125 × 10−3 m².

One detail turned out to matter as much as the data: where the limiter acts. Capping every face transfer on its own recovers little, 38.5 % against 35.2 % for the plain replay, and the diagnostic says why. During cooling the recorded field asks for about 201 J per step and the run passes 35.8 J of it, under a fifth. A cell in the bulk takes the flux in through its lower face and lets almost as much out through the upper one; the balance it keeps is small but the two halves are large, and capping each half separately throws away the part that would have cancelled. Summing the faces first and capping what is left is closer to what $-\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{F}$ means, costs nothing in boundedness, and recovers 45.1 % from the identical data.

Switching the maps off answers that: without them and without the flux the run is meaningless, ending at 329.1 K because nothing carries the inflow away, and with the flux alone it ends at 325.5 K. The channel does transport something in its own right, but roughly an order of magnitude less than the maps carry — it corrects them rather than copying them. The second limit is more basic. This is still a frozen recording: the flux belongs to the frame it was measured in, and the replay applies it to a field that has drifted away from the CFD field of that frame. It gives back the transport the averaging lost. It does not give back the feedback between the temperature it produces and the flow that would answer to it.

15.14 — The number, the picture, and whether the picture is alive

§15.11 left the layer-base mixing at $c = 0.7$ as the closest field of the study, and it still is: 72 % of the cooling and 1.23 K from the reference frames, with an end heterogeneity of 6.61 K against the reference's 6.76 K. Set the difference maps side by side and it is not close — the recorded flux with its operators sits at 3.84 K from the reference at the end of the run, the mixing rule at 1.28 K.

Then measure whether the picture lives. The renewal time of the pattern, the lag at which the field's anomaly loses half the correlation with itself, is 14.7 s in the reference and 19.5 s in the plain replay. The recorded flux keeps it there, 20.6 s. The mixing rule at $c = 0.7$ takes it to 100 s: over the whole cooling phase the same picture is redrawn with a slowly changing shade. Its statistics are the closest of the study and almost nothing in it is made or lost.

The trade between those two ends is not linear, and its middle is the best field of the study. On the last frame its field sits 0.85 K from the reference, against 1.28 K for the mixing alone, which ends a shade too cold. That is the honest state of it. One route reaches the numbers with a field that barely moves; the other keeps the field moving and reaches half the numbers; putting them in the same run trades one for the other rather than adding them. What separates the reference from all of them is still the same thing the difference maps show at the lid: a row of cold fingers that form, fall and disappear, which needs the cooled fluid to change the flow that carries it — and that feedback is exactly what a frozen recording does not contain.

15.15 — What happens when the feedback is switched back on

Every operator in §15.4 to §15.14 works on the scalar while the transport stays frozen. The reference does something none of them can imitate: fluid that the lid has cooled becomes heavy, sinks, and changes the flow that carries it. So the fair question is not "which operator draws plumes" but "what would the replay field do if it were handed back to a solver".

That test needs care, because the replay case cannot be solved as it stands. Two things had gone unnoticed, for a good reason — the replay never solves energy, the temperature lives in the rCFD scalar:

  • the energy model is switched off: the equation list holds only flow;
  • the enthalpy field sits at a uniform 293 K while the temperature field carries the replay values.

Enthalpy is what the energy equation marches on, so the first solver step recomputes the temperature from it and the field is gone. Measured on the replay state: the maximum falls from 332.998 K to 293.000 K within one second of solver time. An earlier attempt at this test therefore returned a run identical, to the last digit, to the run without it — the solver had wiped the field and the replay carried on from its own array.

The transfer is done on the data files instead. The CFD case keeps its own setup — energy equation, wall sink, buoyancy through the density function — and receives the replay's temperature, the enthalpy computed to match it, and the recorded velocity, so the test does not start from rest. Then the solver runs.

Within five seconds the fastest descent grows by a factor of 4.6 and the number of falling cells nine-fold; by twenty seconds it is nearly thirteen times the starting count and the tank has lost 2.8 K, about ten times the rate any operator here reaches. That is the limit, measured rather than asserted. The operators, and the recorded flux beside them, act on a field whose transport cannot answer them, and the descent the reference builds is that answer. The renewal-time column says the same thing: everything driven by the recording stays near 20 s, and the rule that matches the integral numbers best sits at 100 s, while the reference renews in 14.7 s. The burst is a diagnosis, not a method — for those seconds the solver does the work, which is the cost the replay exists to avoid.

Handing it over, and taking it back

The test above ends where the solver ends. What it leaves open is what the replay does with the field afterwards, and answering that needs the pause to happen inside a running replay: the episode loop stops at 315 s, the solver takes the field for twenty seconds, and the replay carries on from what it produced.

Two things make that possible. The replay now writes the enthalpy that matches its temperature at every step, so the state the solver inherits is consistent instead of a uniform 293 K, and the energy equation is switched on in the case before the loop starts. At the first step after the pause the whole field, 85 625 cells, is read back out of the solver into the replay data.

The hand-over works. The field the solver returns is 3.6 K colder than the one it received, 313.769 against 317.383 K, with the cells descending faster than 2 mm/s grown from 1 001 to 4 643 — and the replay picks that up: one step after the pause it carries 314.584 K where the same run without the pause carries 317.344 K.

Then the replay gives it back. By $t = 400$ s the two runs are level, 309.34 against 308.79 K, with the burst run now marginally the warmer of the two; at the end it reaches 302.951 K against 302.754 K, a capture of 45.9 % against 47.8 %. Twenty seconds of real physics leave no lasting trace.

That is the cleanest statement of the limit this study can make. The cooling and the falling structure are not missing because the replay was never given them — they were given, in full, and the frozen transport returned the field to its own trajectory within about a minute of replay time. A mechanism cannot be handed over once. It has to be present while the field evolves, and that is the one thing a recording, of any channel, does not carry.

A property worth recording

Reading the enthalpy field against the temperature field gives the case's own specific heat: $H = c_p\,(T - 298.15)$ with $c_p = 1006.43$ J/kg K, and the same value in the CFD case and in the preparation. The material named water carries the specific heat, the thermal conductivity (0.0242 W/m K) and the molecular weight (28.966 kg/kmol) of air; only the density, through the temperature-dependent function, and the viscosity belong to water. It disturbs no comparison here, since both sides of every comparison share it, and it cancels in the recorded flux, where the same $c_p$ appears in the stored flux and in the cell heat capacity that turns it back into a temperature. It does matter for any statement about the fluid itself, and for the diffusion floor, which on these properties is 0.22 mm rather than the 0.53 mm quoted for molecular water in §15.12.
